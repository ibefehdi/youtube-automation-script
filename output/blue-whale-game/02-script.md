On a November night in Russia’s Far East, a sixteen-year-old posted a selfie. A black scarf covered her mouth. She looked straight into the camera. Under the picture she wrote two words that would outlive her: nya bye.

The next day she was gone.

Her real name was Renata Kambolina. Online she called herself Rina Palenkova. She lived in Ussuriysk, a city near Vladivostok, studied computer networks at a technical college, and played drums in a student rock band. Investigators later closed her case as a suicide. They did not prove that anyone had ordered it. They did not prove that she had been playing a game.

What they could not close was the internet.

Photographs from the railway leaked onto an imageboard. Users argued over whether she was even dead. Then they found her page. They sent her friends money for more pictures. They filmed her grave. Her mother would later say it was those leaked images — not a proved game — that started the hysteria. In certain VKontakte chat rooms — the kind where teenagers talked about school, crushes, depression, and horror stories that claimed to be true — her face became an icon. People who had never met her treated her last caption like a password.

Daria Radchenko, a researcher who studied those rooms, put it plainly. Nobody actually knew the true story behind her death. That did not stop the story from growing. The chats already lived on a blurry line. A good scare, like a horror film that swears it is based on true events, only needs a sliver of something real. Renata’s selfie was that sliver.

On Christmas Day, in the city of Ryazan, a twelve-year-old named Angelina Davydova died. A little over two weeks later, another girl from the same city, Diana Kuznetsova, died too. When their parents opened the accounts the girls had left behind, they found something that felt like a pattern: the same kinds of groups, drawings of Rina, talk of suicide, and again and again, blue whales.

Whales that beach themselves. A melancholy animal floating over a night city. Lyrics from a Russian rock band about a whale that cannot break through a net. No one could agree why the image stuck. It stuck anyway.

One of those fathers, Sergey Pestov, started counting. He used news stories and open sources. He built a list of cases he believed were connected to the groups. The number he reached was one hundred and thirty. He founded a group called Saving Children from Cybercrime. A brochure from that effort hinted that foreign intelligence might be destroying Russian children from the inside.

On the sixteenth of May, 2016, that number left the kitchen table and hit a front page.

Novaya Gazeta, an independent newspaper, published a long investigation by Galina Mursalieva titled Groups of Death. It described communities with names like Ocean Whales and f57. Inside those groups, the article said, so-called curators assigned about fifty tasks over fifty days. Wake up in the night. Watch a horror film. Stand on a ledge. Cut a whale into your skin. On the last day, the player was told to die.

The paper estimated that one hundred and thirty children might have killed themselves between November 2015 and April 2016 because of those groups. Editors told every parent to read it, to save their children from a fateful step.

Within days, the story had been viewed on the order of one to two million times. A regional governor compared the thing to the Islamic State. Russia’s internet watchdog said it would check the facts. A senator demanded new criminal law. Five days after publication, investigators in St. Petersburg — the city where VKontakte is registered — opened a criminal case. The paper said it had already passed material to the authorities. It wanted them goaded into action.

It felt like an open-and-shut horror. A secret adult network. A checklist. A body count. Some critics accused the newspaper of handing the Kremlin a pretext to police the internet more tightly. The editor, Dmitry Muratov, denied any collusion. Adolescent suicide, almost everyone agreed, was a real emergency. The fight was over whether a game was the reason.

The next day, another newsroom asked a quieter question.

Meduza published five problems with the piece. Membership in a sad group does not prove the group caused a death. People in pain go looking for rooms that speak their language. The reverse is just as plausible: the group did not create the thought; the thought found the group. One hundred and thirty was the author’s own count, not an official statistic. The article had not asked VKontakte for comment. It had not asked the police. It had not sat with independent suicide researchers. The only psychologist quoted in the package, Meduza noted, was the journalist’s son. Another outlet, Lenta.ru, described a less cinematic picture: many of the people running the pages were not much older than their members, chasing status or advertising money, not running a sect.

Mursalieva later told the BBC the number came from more than two hundred parents, not one father, and that she had worked the story for months because she thought investigators were not taking it seriously. She asked a question that still sits at the center of this case. Who could confirm it? How can the cause of a suicide ever be proved?

Here is what did not need proving. Russia already had one of the highest rates of adolescent suicide in the world — more than three times the global average, according to figures UNICEF and the World Health Organization had been publishing for years. Prosecutors said most of those deaths were tied to family conflict, school, fear of adults, and a kind of social coldness. National suicide numbers overall had been falling. The internet, researchers said, can be a trigger for someone already in danger. It is rarely the whole cause.

That is a harder story to put on a front page than a game with a final level.

Then a young man walked into the myth and made it look simple.

Philipp Budeikin was twenty-one. Online he called himself Philipp Lis — the Fox. He had started a psychology degree and not finished it. He made witch-house music, the kind with occult textures and a taste for shock. He ran VKontakte publics full of suicide talk and Rina’s face. When the cameras found him, he spoke like a prophet. There are people, he said, and then there is biodegradable waste. He was cleansing society. He said he had invented the game in 2013 under the name f57, from the start of Philipp and the last two digits of his phone. He claimed, in one interview, involvement in seventeen deaths.

In November 2016 they put him in Kresty Prison in St. Petersburg. Early reports talked about fifteen charges. Within a month, almost all of them had fallen away.

Friends told investigative journalist Evgeny Berg a smaller story. Budeikin stuffed groups with the darkest content he could find because darkness gathers followers, and followers can be pointed at a music page. On VKontakte that is not rare. It is a business model.

Berg had felt the first article in his body. He remembered a gloomy May morning and the thought that a man was out there trying to kill children. Then he sat with Pestov, and he watched where the number one hundred and thirty had actually come from.

On the tenth of May, 2017, Budeikin pleaded guilty. On the seventeenth of July, a district court in Tobolsk sentenced him to three years and four months in a penal settlement. The court did not convict him of inventing a worldwide app. It did not convict him of one hundred and thirty deaths. It found two episodes. He had pushed a fifteen-year-old in Astrakhan toward suicide. Security services reached her first. He had driven a girl in the Tyumen region to an attempt that June. Doctors saved her.

Both of them lived.

The Investigative Committee had spoken, at arrest, of fifteen dead and five saved. The verdict was two attempts. That gap is the whole story, if you are willing to sit with it.

The same summer, the State Duma voted four hundred and eight to zero for a law that made it a serious crime to induce a minor to suicide and to run pro-suicide groups online. The president signed it in June. The day Budeikin was sentenced, deputies moved to raise the maximum from six years to fifteen. A panic had become a statute.

While the law moved, folklorists did something journalists and parents had not done in the same way. They logged in.

Alexandra Arkhipova and her colleagues entered the groups that were supposed to be hunting children. The curators they met were not masterminds. They were twelve, thirteen, fourteen. Kids who had read the newspapers. One account that claimed to be a curator belonged, on a closer look, to a twelve-year-old at a Moscow school, posting since she was nine. They sent the script the papers had printed. Cut a whale. We will find you by your IP. We will kill your parents. The researchers did nothing. The threats piled up. Then nothing happened. The game, for them, ended.

Psychiatrists who advised the team told Arkhipova that a video on the internet does not, by itself, drive a person to suicide. What they were watching looked like something older than an app. A moral panic. Adults searching for an enemy they could name. Teenagers learning, from the news, a new way to frighten a classroom.

In all these groups, Arkhipova said, people were waiting for this game. This game never starts.

VKontakte later said Blue Whale hashtags traced to tens of thousands of bots. A safer-internet researcher in Bulgaria counted hundreds of thousands of hashtag uses, much of it automated. There was never a store listing. Never a verified platform. Never a recovered master document of fifty official tasks. The lists that circled the world were folklore wearing the costume of a leaked rulebook.

None of that stopped the whale from leaving Russia.

In Georgia, in the United States, a sixteen-year-old girl died. Only later, after her family heard the story, did her metre-high school paintings of blue whales seem to mean something. Media named a boy, Isaiah Gonzalez. In Hyderabad, a nineteen-year-old. In Irkutsk, two girls, fifteen and sixteen, Yulia Konstantinova and Veronika Volkova, died after a fall from a high building. Days earlier, Konstantinova had posted a whale on Instagram.

Authorities opened files. They did not publish a finding that a central game had issued a final order. A drawing is not a confession. A hashtag is not a verdict.

By 2017 the panic had a second capital: India.

Schools called assemblies. A principal in Punjab told sixteen-year-olds that the first step must never be taken, as if the whale were a drug. One school asked students to wear short sleeves so staff could check their arms for marks. Uttar Pradesh banned smartphones on campus. The government wrote to Facebook, Google, Instagram, and WhatsApp and asked them to remove links to a thing that was not an app. High courts took petitions. The Supreme Court heard the fear called a national problem. An additional solicitor general told the bench that about twenty-eight cases had been reported.

Parents needed a name for an unbearable death. The whale was a name.

Internet researcher Sunil Abraham said the school lectures were advertising. We are going through a moral panic, he told the BBC. The response is moral education. It discounts why people are suicidal. A Delhi psychiatrist, Dr Achal Bhagat, said he spoke to young people every day and had not seen a single Blue Whale case. People join narratives, he said, to explain their experiences. Suicide was already the second most common cause of death among Indian youth. The country had no national suicide-prevention programme worth the name.

In January 2018, the Indian government answered its own scare. A CERT-In committee chaired by its director general had gone through internet logs, devices, call records, social media, other forensics, and interviews with people who had been pulled back from the edge. Minister of State Hansraj Ahir told parliament the result in a written reply. Involvement of the Blue Whale Challenge could not be established in any of those incidents. The scare had produced court hearings, school rituals, and takedown letters. It had not produced a proved killer app.

The United Kingdom’s Safer Internet Centre had already called the original story a sensationalised fake news story. Fact-checkers could not verify the specific claims. A research center that studies cyberbullying wrote that not a single suicide had been confirmed as linked to the challenge. There was no Blue Whale in the app store. There never had been. Calling it a game, one later academic paper argued, gave a rumour a body it did not earn, and made the rumour easier for a teenager to want to find.

Russia’s own Interior Ministry, in March 2017, put the influence of so-called death groups at about one percent of adolescent suicides. The children’s ombudsman, in the same season, blamed those groups for a rise in the official numbers. Same country. Opposite stories. The louder one traveled farther.

So what was real?

This was real: teenagers in pain found rooms that talked about dying, and some of those rooms were cruel. This was real: an administrator used that cruelty, talked about human beings as waste, and a court found he had pushed two girls toward the edge. This was real: a newspaper story with a round number and a monster was easier to share than family violence, alcohol, shame, and a health system that does not know how to speak to children.

This was also real: after the story went around the world, children acted it out. Not because a hidden organization had translated a manual into a dozen languages — investigators never produced that organization — but because the news had handed them a script. Copy the whale. Claim you have a curator. Scare your class. If you are already drowning, the script gives your drowning a plot.

Prevention researchers have a name for that risk. They warn reporters not to detail methods, not to make suicide look like a puzzle you can win. A study of American articles on Blue Whale later found that most of them broke those rules.

There is a temptation, at the end of a story like this, to say it was all a hoax, and walk away. That would be another kind of lie. Renata Kambolina is still gone. Angelina Davydova is still gone. The girls in Ryazan and Irkutsk are still gone. Their parents still opened phones and looked for a reason. Some of them were handed a villain because a villain is something you can hate. A statistic about family conflict is not.

Budeikin went to a penal settlement. The law got harsher. Schools held more assemblies. In Brazil, volunteers invented kinder counter-challenges with other animals, as if a better game could overwrite a bad one. The whale, as a searchable panic, receded. Other names would come later, the way these names always do.

The first photograph is still the one that holds. A girl on a winter street. Two words under her face. A country that needed the internet to be the killer, because the other reasons were older, closer, and harder to arrest.

The game, as it was first sold to the world — a perfect fifty-day machine, a secret adult army, a confirmed mountain of bodies — has not been shown to exist.

The question it left is simpler than a conspiracy, and worse. If you take the bogeyman away, what are you willing to look at instead?
