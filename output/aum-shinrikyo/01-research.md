### VIDEO TITLE

The Cult That Brought Nerve Gas Onto Tokyo’s Morning Trains

### VIDEO TOPIC / CASE NAME

Aum Shinrikyo (オウム真理教) / Tokyo subway sarin attack (地下鉄サリン事件), 20 March 1995 / Matsumoto sarin attack, 27 June 1994 / Sakamoto family murder, 5 November 1989 / Shoko Asahara (Chizuo Matsumoto)

### ONE-SENTENCE LOGLINE

A yoga-and-doomsday movement led by Shoko Asahara built a private chemical-weapons program, murdered critics and neighbors with sarin and VX, then released liquid nerve agent on Tokyo’s rush-hour subway on 20 March 1995—killing 14 people, injuring thousands, and ending with Asahara’s execution in 2018 while successor groups still face Japanese surveillance.

### WHY THIS STORY WORKS

- Core emotion / hook: Hubris and institutional lag. A legally recognized religion hired elite university graduates, built a multimillion-dollar chemical plant in the shadow of Mount Fuji, and attacked the capital’s circulatory system while police were already preparing a raid.
- Why it fits a faceless documentary: The story lives in trains, newspaper-wrapped plastic bags, plastic umbrellas, Satyan-7’s industrial corridors, courtrooms, and quiet memorials—not gore. Documents, stations, and ritual spaces carry the narrative.
- What is visually depictable: 1980s Tokyo yoga studios; Kamikuishiki / Fujigamine compound buildings; Satyan-7 exterior; Matsumoto residential streets at night; Yokohama apartment of the Sakamoto family; Chiyoda / Marunouchi / Hibiya Line cars at rush hour; Kasumigaseki Station platforms; JGSDF chemical units in protective gear; Tokyo Detention House; Aleph facility inspections decades later.
- Suggested video length: 15–18 minutes.
- What makes this story different from the most obvious YouTube version: Most recaps jump straight to 20 March 1995. The fuller record starts with the Sakamoto murders in 1989, failed biological weapons, the Matsumoto “experiment,” tipoff letters predicting a subway attack, and a cult that still exists under new names.

### CONFIRMED FACTS

- Aum Shinrikyo was founded in February 1984 by Chizuo Matsumoto (born 2 March 1955), who later took the name Shoko Asahara. The early group was called Aum Shinsen no Kai (Aum Divine Beings’ Society) and began as a yoga and meditation circle. (Source: Nippon.com Aum timeline; Wikipedia Aum Shinrikyo / Shoko Asahara pages used as pointers to Japanese court and news records.)
- In June 1987 the group renamed itself Aum Shinrikyo—“Supreme Truth.” Doctrine mixed Indian and Tibetan Buddhist ideas, Hindu imagery (especially Shiva), and later apocalyptic Christian motifs. Asahara claimed enlightenment and later presented himself as Christ and as a figure destined to build a utopian “Kingdom of Shambhala.” (Source: BBC, 6 July 2018; congressional / GlobalSecurity Aum case study, 1995.)
- August 1989: Tokyo Metropolitan Government granted Aum official religious corporation status, with tax advantages and reduced oversight. Membership and assets grew rapidly; by the early 1990s estimates of membership ran into the tens of thousands worldwide, with major concentrations in Japan and Russia. (Source: Wikipedia Tokyo subway / Aum pages summarizing Japanese government and news figures; BBC on peak following.)
- November 1989: Lawyer Tsutsumi Sakamoto (working on a class-action case against Aum), his wife Satoko, and infant son Tatsuhiko were murdered in their Yokohama apartment by Aum members including Hideo Murai, Satoru Hashimoto, Tomomasa Nakagawa, and Kazuaki Okazaki. Bodies were buried and not recovered until September 1995, after arrests related to the subway attack. Courts later tied the order to Asahara. (Source: Wikipedia Sakamoto family murder; Nippon.com timeline; CNN, July 2018.)
- February 1990: Asahara’s Shinrito (“Truth Party”) fielded candidates for the Diet and was crushed at the polls. Afterward Asahara blamed external conspiracies and accelerated plans for biological and chemical weapons. (Source: Wikipedia Tokyo subway attack background; GlobalSecurity / U.S. Senate staff case study.)
- 1990–1993: Biological weapons efforts under Seiichi Endo—botulinum toxin and anthrax—largely failed. Spraying attacks on U.S. bases, Narita Airport, the Diet, the Imperial Palace, and other targets produced foul smells but no mass casualties. Anthrax used was reportedly a Sterne vaccine strain. (Source: Wikipedia attack background; CNAS / Danzig et al., *Aum Shinrikyo: Insights Into How Terrorists Develop Biological and Chemical Weapons*.)
- From late 1992, chemist Masami Tsuchiya, tasked by Science and Technology Minister Hideo Murai, synthesized small amounts of sarin. Aum then built Satyan-7 (also spelled Satyam / Satyan No. 7), a chemical plant at the Kamikuishiki compound in Yamanashi Prefecture, intended for mass production (prosecutors later cited plans for tens of tons). UN estimates later put the facility’s value around $30 million. (Source: GlobalSecurity congressional case study; CNAS / Danzig; USF Journal of Strategic Security on Aum chemical efforts.)
- September 1993: Asahara and followers traveled to Banjawarn Station, a large property in Western Australia purchased by Aum; Australian investigations after 1995 found evidence consistent with chemical testing and leftover hazardous chemicals. (Source: Wikipedia Tokyo subway background summarizing AFP investigation.)
- 27 June 1994: Matsumoto sarin attack. Aum members released sarin from a modified truck overnight in a residential neighborhood of Matsumoto, Nagano Prefecture, targeting judges in a land dispute involving the cult. Eight people ultimately died (seven soon after; one later), and hundreds were injured or sought care. Neighbor Yoshiyuki Kōno was wrongly suspected for a time. (Source: Wikipedia; OPCW forensic overview; BBC.)
- September 1994: Anonymous letters to Japanese media claimed Aum responsibility for Matsumoto and warned that releasing sarin indoors—e.g., in a crowded subway—would be far worse. (Source: Wikipedia Tokyo subway attack.)
- Late 1994–early 1995: Aum also used VX and other agents in targeted attacks, including the December 1994 murder of Tadahito Hamaguchi in Osaka with VX and attacks on anti-Aum activists and critics. (Source: Wikipedia chemical-attack table; Japan Times on Endo’s convictions.)
- January 1995: Yomiuri Shimbun published leaked reporting that soil near Aum facilities showed sarin-related degradation products. Aum began destroying or hiding chemical stocks. (Source: Wikipedia.)
- March 1995: Police planned a major raid for 22 March, partly tied to a kidnapping case and chemical evidence. Cult members inside the Self-Defense Forces tipped Asahara. He ordered a subway attack for the morning of 20 March to disrupt police and/or accelerate his apocalyptic scenario. (Source: Wikipedia; Nikkei Asia, March 2025 retrospective.)
- 18–19 March 1995: Fresh sarin was produced in haste for the subway attack; impurity left it brownish-red rather than colorless. Roughly 30 kg was made and packed into plastic bags. Purity was lower than Matsumoto’s batch. (Source: Nikkei Asia, 2025; Wikipedia.)
- 20 March 1995, ~8:00 a.m. JST: Five Aum members—Ikuo Hayashi, Kenichi Hirose, Masato Yokoyama, Toru Toyoda, and Yasuo Hayashi—boarded trains on the Chiyoda, Marunouchi, and Hibiya lines (then Teito Rapid Transit Authority / Tokyo Metro lines), dropped newspaper-wrapped plastic bags of liquid sarin, and punctured them with sharpened umbrella tips before fleeing to getaway drivers. Targets were trains passing near Kasumigaseki and Nagatachō, the government district. (Source: Nikkei Asia; Wikipedia perpetrator table; OPCW.)
- Casualties of the subway attack: early official tallies listed 12 dead; later reviews recognized 13, then 14 after Sachiko Asakawa died in March 2020 from long-term effects of the poisoning. Thousands sought care—commonly cited figures include ~5,500 people treated and broader injury counts over 6,000 depending on methodology. St. Luke’s International Hospital alone treated hundreds. (Source: Wikipedia aftermath; Nikkei Asia; OPCW; BBC for earlier “13” framing.)
- Immediate response: Fire and police initially misread the event; Forensic Science Laboratory confirmed sarin by late morning using GC/MS, aided by Matsumoto experience. JGSDF chemical units later decontaminated trains and stations. (Source: OPCW; Wikipedia emergency-services section.)
- 22 March 1995 onward: Massive raids on Kamikuishiki and other facilities. Over subsequent months, hundreds of members were arrested. Asahara was arrested in May 1995. Hideo Murai was fatally stabbed by an assassin outside Aum’s Tokyo office on 23 April 1995 while cameras rolled. (Source: BBC; CNN; contemporaneous Japanese news summaries.)
- Legal outcomes: Of 189 Aum members indicted in related cases, 13 received death sentences (including Asahara), others life or lesser terms. Asahara was sentenced to death on 27 February 2004; final appeals failed by 2006, with executions delayed until remaining fugitive cases concluded. (Source: Wikipedia aftermath; Japan Times on Endo.)
- 6 July 2018: Asahara and six others—Tomomasa Nakagawa, Tomomitsu Niimi, Kiyohide Hayakawa, Yoshihiro Inoue, Seiichi Endo, and Masami Tsuchiya—were hanged. Six more were executed on 26 July 2018, including several subway attackers. (Source: BBC; CNN; Wikipedia Shoko Asahara / subway attack.)
- Successor organizations: The group renamed itself Aleph in 2000; Fumihiro Joyu’s Hikari no Wa split in 2007; another faction (“Yamada-ra no Shudan”) appeared later. Japan’s Public Security Intelligence Agency continues surveillance and facility inspections; photos of Asahara/Matsumoto have still been found in facilities years after the executions. Aleph and related entities remain designated terrorist organizations by the U.S. and other countries even while operating under domestic Japanese religious-group monitoring rules. (Source: Nippon.com; Yomiuri / Japan News, March 2025; Jiji, 2020; BBC.)

### INVESTIGATION, MEDIA & INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE

- Matsumoto was initially mishandled; a local man was wrongly suspected while Aum escaped clear attribution until after March 1995.
- Pre-subway, police already planned a 22 March raid; the subway attack forced an even larger national mobilization—among the largest single-case investigations in Japanese history.
- Hospitals learned from Matsumoto; atropine and 2-PAM were used once sarin was identified; antidote shortages required emergency resupply from outside Tokyo.
- Some religion scholars initially defended Aum’s possible innocence; a 1995 visit by foreign advocates funded by Aum damaged scholarly reputations when evidence of Satyan-7 became undeniable (Ian Reader’s later analysis).
- Diet later passed monitoring laws tailored to groups involved in indiscriminate mass murder; Aum lost religious-corporation privileges; full outlawing was debated but successor groups persist under surveillance.

### CONFLICTING ACCOUNTS & CONTRADICTIONS

- **Death toll framing:** Early reports and some 2018 international coverage said 12 or 13 subway deaths; Japanese tallies later recognized 14. Injury counts vary (≈5,000 treated vs. “over 6,000”) depending on whether worried-well hospital visits are included.
- **Asahara’s direct orders:** Courts found him guilty on major counts including Sakamoto and the sarin attacks; Asahara and some defense arguments denied or minimized personal direction, casting blame-shifting onto disciples. Disciples’ testimony was central.
- **Purpose of 20 March attack:** Disruption of the imminent police raid is well supported; whether Asahara also intended to “trigger” prophesied Armageddon is interpretive but consistent with cult doctrine reported in trials and journalism.
- **Successor sincerity:** Aleph / Hikari no Wa publicly renounce violence; security agencies say Asahara’s image and doctrine still appear inside facilities.

### THEORIES

- **Raid-preemption theory (widely accepted):** Attack timed to cripple police response before 22 March raid — supported by tipoffs from SDF-linked members and Asahara’s orders as established in investigations.
- **Apocalypse acceleration (doctrinal):** Killing outsiders as “poa” / salvation — reflected in cult teaching and some court characterizations; hard to separate from strategic panic.
- **Early scholarly “frame-up” claims (discredited):** 1995 foreign advocates suggested Aum lacked sarin capacity; contradicted by Satyan-7 evidence and convictions.

### HUMAN COST & AFTERMATH

- Fourteen dead from the subway attack (as later counted); eight dead from Matsumoto; Sakamoto family of three; additional targeted murder victims (e.g., Hamaguchi).
- Thousands with lasting eye damage, trauma, and PTSD; victims’ compensation law (2008) processed thousands of applications.
- Families such as Shizue Takahashi’s became long-term advocates through decades of trials.
- Public rubbish bins were widely removed across Japan partly due to terror-security concerns after 1995.
- Japan’s sense of domestic safety was permanently altered; the attack remains the country’s deadliest modern terrorism case.

### MYTHS VS REALITY

- **Myth:** Aum was a fringe handful of fanatics with no real science. **Reality:** It recruited physicians, physicists, and chemists from top universities and spent on the order of tens of millions on a chemical plant.
- **Myth:** The subway attack came from nowhere. **Reality:** Sakamoto (1989), Matsumoto (1994), tipoff letters, and soil chemistry all preceded it.
- **Myth:** Executing Asahara ended the movement. **Reality:** Aleph and splinters still exist under surveillance; devotion to Asahara’s image has been documented in inspections years later.
- **Myth:** Biological weapons were Aum’s main success. **Reality:** Bio programs largely failed; chemical weapons caused the mass casualties.

### SCRIPT-READY RESEARCH PACK

**Video Title:** The Cult That Brought Nerve Gas Onto Tokyo’s Morning Trains  
**Case:** Aum Shinrikyo / Tokyo subway sarin attack (20 March 1995) and precursor crimes (Sakamoto 1989; Matsumoto 1994)

Shoko Asahara (born Chizuo Matsumoto, 1955) built Aum from a 1984 yoga circle into a doomsday religion granted legal corporate status in 1989. The group mixed Buddhist, Hindu, and apocalyptic Christian ideas; taught that killing could save doomed souls (“poa”); and recruited elite graduates into a shadow “government” with science and chemical ministries.

In November 1989 Aum members murdered lawyer Tsutsumi Sakamoto, his wife Satoko, and their baby son Tatsuhiko in Yokohama to stop litigation against the cult. Bodies were hidden until 1995.

After a failed 1990 political bid, Aum pursued biological weapons (botulinum, anthrax) that largely failed, then chemical weapons. Chemist Masami Tsuchiya produced sarin; Satyan-7 at Kamikuishiki was built for mass production. On 27 June 1994 Aum released sarin in Matsumoto, killing eight and injuring hundreds—initially blamed on a local resident. Tipoff letters later warned a subway release would be worse. VX assassinations and other attacks followed. Soil evidence of sarin chemistry near Aum facilities leaked in January 1995.

Warned of a 22 March 1995 police raid, Asahara ordered a 20 March rush-hour attack on Tokyo subway lines serving the government district. Five men punctured bags of impure liquid sarin with umbrella tips on Chiyoda, Marunouchi, and Hibiya trains. Fourteen people eventually died; thousands were injured. Raids exposed the weapons program. After long trials, Asahara and other leaders were executed in July 2018. Successor groups Aleph and Hikari no Wa remain under Japanese surveillance; Asahara’s image has still appeared in inspected facilities years later.
