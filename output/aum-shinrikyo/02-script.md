On the morning of March 20, 1995, Tokyo’s subway did what it always did. Trains packed with office workers slid through tunnels toward Kasumigaseki, the city’s political heart. People read newspapers. They stood shoulder to shoulder. They trusted the ordinary rhythm of a Monday commute.

Then five men stepped aboard different cars with plastic bags wrapped in newsprint and umbrellas whose tips had been sharpened the night before. Just before their stops, they set the bags on the floor and stabbed them open. A brownish liquid bled out, evaporated, and turned the air itself into a weapon.

Within minutes, commuters were choking, vomiting, and clawing at their eyes. Station attendants collapsed trying to help. Hospitals filled with thousands of people who had simply gone to work. By the time the city understood what had happened, Japan’s deadliest modern terrorist attack was already over—and it had been carried out not by a foreign army, but by a legally recognized religion that called itself Supreme Truth.

This is the story of Aum Shinrikyo, and of the man who built it: Shoko Asahara, born Chizuo Matsumoto.

He entered the world in 1955 in Kumamoto Prefecture, partially blind from birth, educated in schools for the blind, restless for a destiny larger than the life assigned to him. By the mid-1980s he was teaching yoga in Tokyo, collecting seekers who wanted meaning outside Japan’s pressure-cooker prosperity. In February 1984 he founded a circle called Aum Shinsen no Kai. In 1987 it became Aum Shinrikyo. The name promised absolute truth. The doctrine mixed Buddhist training, Hindu imagery—especially the god Shiva—and, later, apocalyptic Christian prophecy. Asahara told followers he was enlightened. Then he told them he was Christ. Then he told them the world was heading for war, and that only his circle would survive to build a utopian kingdom.

For a while, the outside world treated Aum like any other new religion. In August 1989, Tokyo authorities granted it religious corporation status. Tax advantages followed. Membership and money surged. Young engineers, doctors, and graduate students—people with elite degrees and empty spiritual hunger—streamed in. Asahara organized them into a shadow government, complete with ministries. Science. Construction. Healing. Health and welfare. The titles sounded like satire until you saw what those ministries actually built.

Not everyone was enchanted. Families accused the group of cutting children off from home. A Yokohama lawyer named Tsutsumi Sakamoto took on a class-action case for people who wanted their relatives back. He was thorough, public, and dangerous to Aum’s image.

On November 5, 1989, before dawn, Aum members entered the Sakamoto family’s apartment through an unlocked door. Tsutsumi was struck and killed. So was his wife, Satoko. So was their infant son, Tatsuhiko. The killers had come days earlier with syringes and potassium chloride, hoping to seize him at a train station; when a holiday kept the family home, they changed the plan. They hid the bodies in remote ground. For six years, Japan treated the disappearance as an unsolved horror. Only after the subway attack would confessions lead police to the remains. Courts later concluded the murders were committed on Asahara’s orders to stop the lawsuit. It was the cult’s first great blood debt—and a warning of how far “salvation” could stretch.

Inside Aum, Asahara taught a concept often summarized as poa: the idea that killing a person trapped in bad karma could spare them a worse fate. In a closed world, compassion became a vocabulary for murder. Followers who doubted were told their hesitation was spiritual weakness. The more the group sealed itself off, the easier it became to redefine crime as mercy.

In 1990, Asahara tried politics. His Truth Party fielded candidates for Japan’s Diet and was crushed at the polls, winning a humiliating handful of votes. The failure mattered. Afterward he spoke of conspiracies—Jews, Freemasons, rival religions, foreign powers—and turned from ballots to weapons. Followers were sent to harvest bacteria from river soil in Hokkaido, to brew crude botulinum broth in giant fermenters, to spray foul slurry near U.S. naval bases, Narita Airport, the Diet building, and the Imperial Palace. The biological program largely failed. Anthrax stocks appear to have been a vaccine strain incapable of the mass death Asahara imagined. People smelled something rotten. Almost no one got sick. One member even fell into a fermenter and climbed out more embarrassed than poisoned. Failure did not humble Asahara. It redirected him.

Chemists could succeed where microbiologists had not.

Masami Tsuchiya, working under science minister Hideo Murai, produced grams of sarin by late 1992 using a classic multi-step synthesis first mapped in the twentieth century’s chemical-weapons race. Sarin is a nerve agent: colorless when pure, terrifyingly potent, able to disrupt the body’s ability to turn muscles and breath on and off. Aum did not stop at laboratory proof. At the Kamikuishiki compound in Yamanashi Prefecture, in the shadow of Mount Fuji, the group built Satyan-7—a chemical plant prosecutors later said was meant to churn out tens of tons of agent. Corrosion-resistant reactors, mixing flasks under hoods, computer controls, shell companies buying precursors. Later estimates put the facility’s value around thirty million dollars. At peak activity it employed scores of members. Workers got nosebleeds and convulsions when systems leaked. Buckets caught spills. Neighbors complained of strange smells. Aum claimed it was under gas attack by enemies of the truth.

In 1993 Asahara’s people also bought a vast property in Western Australia called Banjawarn Station. They flew in generators, masks, and chemicals, searched for uranium, and left hazards behind when they sold the land in a hurry. After 1995, Australian federal police found residue consistent with chemical experimentation. The apocalypse, in Aum’s imagination, was never only a Japanese story. It was a supply chain.

By 1994, Aum was no longer only rehearsing. Followers tried to assassinate Daisaku Ikeda, leader of the rival movement Soka Gakkai, with truck-mounted sarin systems that malfunctioned and poisoned their own operators. Tomomitsu Niimi nearly died before receiving antidotes from cult doctors. Then, on the night of June 27, Asahara ordered a clearer demonstration. In Matsumoto, Nagano Prefecture, cult members released about thirty liters of sarin from a modified truck into a residential neighborhood, aiming at judges tied to a land dispute involving Aum. Warm air, open windows, sleeping families. The first emergency call came before midnight. Seven people died in the immediate aftermath; an eighth death was later tied to the attack. Hundreds were hurt or sought care. For a time, suspicion fell on a local man, Yoshiyuki Kōno, whose own wife was left comatose by the gas. The cult watched the chaos like data from a field test.

That autumn, anonymous letters to Japanese media claimed responsibility and delivered a cold prediction: Matsumoto had been an open-air experiment. Indoors—on a crowded subway—the results would be far worse. The letters read like a threat dressed as analysis. In hindsight, they were a blueprint.

Aum also branched into other agents. VX, thicker and more persistent than sarin, was used in targeted attacks. In December 1994, a man the cult believed was spying on them, Tadahito Hamaguchi, was sprayed on a street in Osaka by attackers posing as joggers. He died days later. Journalist Shoko Egawa, who had covered the Sakamoto disappearance, was attacked with phosgene through a mail slot. Anti-cult activists and lawyers were hunted with syringes and intimidation. Inside the movement, dissenters vanished into discipline, drugs, and fear. The “Ministry of Healing” ran treatments that included electric shock and truth serums for members whose loyalty wavered.

In January 1995, the Yomiuri Shimbun reported that soil near Aum facilities showed chemicals linked to sarin production and breakdown. The group scrambled to destroy stocks. Police, building a case that also involved a kidnapping of a man connected to an escaped follower, set a major raid for March 22. Two followers with ties to the Self-Defense Forces warned Asahara. He did not wait to be surrounded. He ordered an attack on the morning of March 20—on subway lines feeding Kasumigaseki and Nagatachō, where Japan’s parliament and key agencies sit—hoping to cripple the investigation and, in the cult’s logic, shove history toward the end he had promised.

Sarin for the subway was cooked in a rush on March 18 and 19. Missing the usual precursors after the destruction campaign, the batch came out impure—brownish-red instead of clear. Roughly thirty kilograms were poured into plastic bags. Purity was lower than Matsumoto’s batch. That impurity may have saved thousands of lives. It still killed.

Ten Aum members ran the operation: five attackers, five drivers. Ikuo Hayashi, a former senior physician who had left a respectable hospital career for Asahara’s favor, boarded a Chiyoda Line train wearing a surgical mask, bags wrapped in a leftist newspaper, and punctured one packet as the car neared the city center. Two station attendants later died handling the aftermath at Kasumigaseki. Kenichi Hirose, a physicist from Waseda, forced both bags open on a Marunouchi train so violently he bent his umbrella tip and poisoned himself, then injected atropine in the getaway car. Masato Yokoyama, in a wig and fake glasses, left packets only partly pierced; his train kept running for more than an hour, spreading harm more slowly across the line. Toru Toyoda’s drop on the Hibiya Line was over in about two minutes; panic hit by the next stations. Yasuo Hayashi carried three bags instead of two—an alleged loyalty test after Asahara had suspected him of being a spy—and punctured with brutal thoroughness. At Kodenmachō Station, a passenger kicked a soaked package onto the platform. People died there, not only in the cars.

Emergency services first heard talk of an explosion in a subway yard. Officers walked into contamination before they understood the air. Firefighters and subway staff became victims while trying to help. St. Luke’s International Hospital, near Tsukiji, and dozens of other hospitals faced a flood of patients—critical, severe, and a much larger wave of the “worried well” who arrived terrified after hearing the news. By late morning, Tokyo’s forensic labs confirmed sarin with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, drawing on hard lessons from Matsumoto. A Matsumoto physician faxed treatment guidance to Tokyo hospitals. Doctors reached for atropine and 2-PAM. Antidotes had to be rushed in from outside the capital, some carried toward Tokyo on express trains. The Ground Self-Defense Force eventually rolled in with chemical units to scrub trains and stations, while police prepared the largest single-case mobilization many officers had ever seen.

On the day of the attack, eight people died. More died later. Early official tallies said twelve. Later reviews recognized thirteen, then fourteen after Sachiko Asakawa, bedridden for twenty-five years, died in March 2020 from complications of the poisoning. Thousands were injured; reports commonly cite roughly fifty-five hundred people treated, with broader counts running higher. Surveys years afterward found survivors still afraid of trains, still living with damaged vision, insecurity, and post-traumatic stress. A 2008 compensation law processed thousands of claims because the attack had been aimed at the Japanese state as much as at random citizens.

Two days after the gassing, police poured into Kamikuishiki backed by careful planning and Self-Defense Force contingency support. They expected possible armed resistance, even airborne dispersal from cult aircraft. Instead they met unarmed protest and empty labs where leaders had fled. Still, the infrastructure of a private weapons program was undeniable. Arrests mounted into the hundreds. In April, Hideo Murai was stabbed to death outside an Aum office while television cameras watched—another sudden wound in a country trying to understand how this had been built in plain sight. In May, Asahara himself was pulled from hiding inside the compound’s warren of rooms.

Some scholars of religion, cultivated by Aum before the attack, initially suggested the group might be a scapegoat. A short-lived visit by foreign advocates, travel paid by Aum, produced claims that the cult could not have made the sarin—claims that collapsed against Satyan-7’s evidence and the confessions already stacking up. The episode damaged public trust in experts who seemed to defend the indefensible.

Trials lasted years. Of one hundred eighty-nine members indicted across the cult’s crimes, thirteen received death sentences. Others got life. Ikuo Hayashi’s cooperation helped map the conspiracy and spared him the gallows. Asahara was sentenced to death in February 2004 after what Japanese media called a trial of the century. Appeals dragged through psychiatric questions and procedure. Executions waited until the last major fugitives were caught—Katsuya Takahashi, a getaway driver, was arrested in 2012 near a Tokyo comic café, ending years of wanted posters.

On July 6, 2018, Japan hanged Asahara and six senior followers, including chemist Masami Tsuchiya and bioweapons figure Seiichi Endo. Later that month, more condemned members—including several who had punctured bags on the trains—were executed as well. Shizue Takahashi, whose husband died as a subway worker dealing with the attack’s aftermath, said Asahara deserved death and that she shed no tears for him. Film director Atsushi Sakahara, injured in the attack, said he felt the world become slightly brighter, even if forgetting was impossible.

Aum did not simply vanish. It lost religious-corporation privileges, shed assets under legal pressure, and rebranded. In 2000 the main body took the name Aleph. In 2007, former spokesman Fumihiro Joyu formed a splinter called Hikari no Wa—the Ring of Light. Another faction emerged later. Japanese law stopped short of a total ban, but the Diet gave the Public Security Intelligence Agency powers to monitor groups tied to indiscriminate mass murder and charismatic control. Inspectors still enter facilities. As recently as the thirtieth-anniversary reporting in 2025, authorities described successor groups that obscure assets, move activity into affiliated companies, and, in some training halls, still keep the founder’s photograph near the lessons.

Abroad, Aum and its heirs remain listed as terrorist organizations by the United States and other governments. At home, the story sits in a more uncomfortable place: a democracy that once stamped a doomsday factory with the legal status of a religion, then spent decades prosecuting what that status helped conceal.

There is a temptation to tell this only as a story about madmen and magic. The record is colder. Aum hired competence. It built Satyan-7. It learned from Matsumoto. It received a tip about a police raid and chose a Monday morning subway as its battlefield. The bags were cheap. The umbrellas were plastic. The ideology was mystical. The logistics were practical.

Haruki Murakami, interviewing survivors for his book Underground, argued that Japanese media had obsessed over the cult’s grotesque theater and underplayed the ordinary people whose commute became a crime scene. Their accounts are the other half of the truth: burned eyes, stalled trains, strangers sharing water, station staff who stayed when they could have run, a city discovering that safety had been a habit, not a guarantee. Murakami later listened to current and former Aum members too, mapping the parallel world that had made the attack thinkable.

Thirty years on, the platforms are clean again. Public rubbish bins stayed scarce across Japan for years afterward, a quiet redesign of daily life in the name of security. Memorials appear each March. Victims’ families continue to press successor groups for accountability that outlasts a single hanging. And the central fact remains as stark as it was at eight in the morning on that Monday: a cult that preached the end of the world decided not to wait for prophecy. It manufactured the ending, poured it into plastic, and set it on the floor of a rush-hour train.
