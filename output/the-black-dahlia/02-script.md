On the morning of January 15, 1947, a mother named Betty Bersinger was walking her child through a quiet stretch of Los Angeles when something in a vacant lot stopped her cold. At first the shape on the grass looked almost unreal, pale as a mannequin left out overnight. Then the truth arrived. What she had found was the body of a young woman, severed cleanly at the waist, drained of blood, washed, and arranged with a care that felt deliberate. There was almost no blood on the ground. Whoever had done this had killed her somewhere else, cleaned her, and placed her here for the city to discover.

Her name was Elizabeth Short. She was twenty-two years old. Within hours, newspapers would rename her the Black Dahlia, and that invented title would eclipse the person she actually was. Nearly eighty years later, the Los Angeles Police Department has still never charged anyone with her murder. The FBI still lists the case among its famous unsolved histories. The body was identified. The killer was not.

Elizabeth Short was born on July 29, 1924, in Boston’s Hyde Park neighborhood, the third of five daughters. The family settled in Medford, Massachusetts. Her father, Cleo, built miniature golf courses until the crash of 1929 wrecked his savings. In 1930 his car was found abandoned on the Charlestown Bridge, and people assumed he had jumped into the Charles River. Phoebe Short, believing herself a widow, went to work as a bookkeeper and raised the girls alone. Years later the family learned the truth. Cleo was alive. He had started over in California.

Elizabeth was often sick. Bronchitis and severe asthma marked her childhood. After lung surgery around age fifteen, doctors urged milder winters, so Phoebe sent her to Miami. She left Medford High School during her sophomore year. She was not famous, not connected, and not yet a myth. She was a teenager trying to breathe easier in Florida heat.

In late 1942, eighteen-year-old Elizabeth traveled west to live with the father she barely knew. He worked at the Mare Island Naval Shipyard near Vallejo. The reunion did not last. By January 1943 she had moved out. She took work at the Base Exchange at Camp Cooke, near Lompoc. That September, in Santa Barbara, she was arrested for underage drinking. The arrest put her fingerprints in federal files. Those prints would identify her body four years later in under an hour.

After the arrest, authorities wanted her sent east. She went back to Florida instead. There she met Major Matthew Michael Gordon Jr., an Army Air Forces officer. Friends later said he wrote from overseas proposing marriage after surviving a plane crash in India. She said she accepted. He died in a second crash on August 10, 1945. Whether every detail of that romance was exactly as she told it, the loss sits in the record as part of the story she carried.

In July 1946 she returned to Southern California to visit an acquaintance stationed in Long Beach. The last six months of her life unfolded in and around Los Angeles. She worked as a waitress. She rented a room behind the Florentine Gardens on Hollywood Boulevard. Papers would later call her an aspiring actress, a Hollywood hopeful chasing the screen. The harder fact is simpler. She had no known acting credits. She was a young woman drifting through a city full of men in uniform, nightclubs, borrowed couches, and temporary rooms.

In December 1946 she spent time in San Diego. According to later reporting, a family took her in after finding her seeking shelter in a late-night theater. One of the men she met was Robert Manley, a twenty-five-year-old married salesman with red hair. People called him Red. On January 9, 1947, Manley drove her back toward Los Angeles. He said they stayed overnight in a motel without intimacy. The next day he helped her check luggage and left her at the Biltmore Hotel downtown. She told him she was supposed to meet a sister visiting from Boston. Some accounts place her using a lobby telephone. Others place her later at the Crown Grill Cocktail Lounge on South Olive Street, a short walk away. Then the confirmed trail ends.

From January 9 to January 15, Elizabeth Short’s movements are a blank that investigators never filled. People who did not know her claimed sightings during that missing week. Police and district attorney investigators checked them. Again and again, the sightings collapsed. Either the witnesses were wrong, or they had seen someone else. The last days of her life remain one of the central open questions of the case.

When Betty Bersinger found the body on South Norton Avenue, between Coliseum Street and West Thirty-Ninth in Leimert Park, the lot was still largely undeveloped. The corpse had been posed: hands above the head, elbows bent, legs apart. The face had been slashed from the corners of the mouth toward the ears, a wound later called a Glasgow smile. Flesh had been cut from the body. The lower half lay about a foot from the upper. Near the scene, detectives noted a heel print amid tire tracks and a cement sack containing watery blood. Herald-Express reporter Aggie Underwood was among the first journalists there. Photographs of the scene entered the machinery of a newspaper war that would devour the investigation.

On January 16, Los Angeles County coroner Frederick Newbarr performed the autopsy. Elizabeth Short was five feet five, one hundred fifteen pounds, with light blue eyes, brown hair, and badly decayed teeth. Ligature marks marked her ankles, wrists, and neck. The body had been completely severed at the waist by transecting the lumbar spine, a cut so orderly that investigators immediately wondered whether the killer had medical training. Bruising along the incision line was minimal, consistent with postmortem mutilation rather than the cause of death. Newbarr concluded she died from hemorrhage and shock tied to facial lacerations and blows to the head. Tests for sperm were negative. Sensational papers would claim she had been tortured for hours. Stronger accounts say police allowed some of that false narrative to circulate while protecting the true medical findings. Claims of cigarette burns and forced ingestion of feces appear in later books and some popular retellings; they are disputed and not treated here as confirmed autopsy facts.

Identification came through the FBI. Fingerprints from the Santa Barbara arrest matched. According to the Bureau’s own history of the case, once Los Angeles sent the prints by Soundphoto, a primitive fax system, the match came back in about fifty-six minutes. The dead woman in the lot was Elizabeth Short of Medford.

Then the press found her mother. Reporters from William Randolph Hearst’s Los Angeles Examiner telephoned Phoebe Short in Boston. According to later recollection by journalist Jim Murray, a reporter began by letting Phoebe believe her daughter had won a beauty contest, extracting personal details before revealing the murder. Murray said he remained appalled by the call decades afterward. The Examiner offered to fly Phoebe west, another move that also served the paper’s scoop. In the days that followed, Hearst papers and rivals turned the killing into a national spectacle. They nicknamed her the Black Dahlia, a phrase linked variously to the 1946 film The Blue Dahlia, to rumors of sheer black clothing, or to a Long Beach drugstore nickname she may or may not have carried in life. District attorney investigators later credited newspaper reporters with inventing or popularizing the name after the murder. Early coverage even floated “Werewolf Murder.” The brand stuck. The woman underneath it began to disappear.

On January 21, a man claiming to be the killer called Examiner editor James Richardson. He congratulated the paper on its coverage, said he would eventually surrender, and told Richardson to expect souvenirs of Beth Short in the mail. Three days later, a manila envelope arrived addressed to the Examiner and other Los Angeles papers. Cut-and-paste newspaper lettering announced that Dahlia’s belongings were inside, with a letter to follow. Inside were her birth certificate, business cards, photographs, scraps of paper with names, and an address book embossed with the name Mark Hansen. The packet had been cleaned with gasoline, the same careful hygiene suggested by the washed body. Partial fingerprints were lifted and sent to the FBI, then compromised in transit and lost as usable evidence. The same day, a handbag and a black suede shoe turned up near a garbage can about two miles from the lot. Those, too, had been wiped with gasoline.

Mark Hansen was a wealthy nightclub and theater owner. Elizabeth had stayed at his home with friends. Roommate Ann Toth told police Short had rejected Hansen’s sexual advances, offering a possible motive. Hansen was investigated and cleared. Robert Manley, the last known companion, was arrested around January 19 and released after polygraph examinations and lack of evidence. Detectives worked through Hansen’s address book and interviewed more than one hundred fifty men. At the height of the hunt, roughly seven hundred fifty investigators from the LAPD and other agencies were pulled into the effort. Councilman Lloyd Davis posted a ten-thousand-dollar reward. Confessions poured in. Most were fantasy. Some false confessors were charged with obstruction.

More letters followed. One, signed in spirit as the Black Dahlia Avenger, promised a surrender on January 29. Police waited. No one came. Another paste-up letter claimed a change of mind and declared the killing justified. The Examiner kept the story on its front page for weeks. By February 1, the Daily News was already saying the case had hit a stone wall.

Because of the precise cuts, police looked hard at anyone with surgical skill. In mid-February they obtained student lists from the University of Southern California Medical School, near the dump site. Background checks produced nothing that solved the case. Captain Jack Donahue told the press he believed the murder happened in some remote building or shack outside the city’s center, after which the body was transported to Norton Avenue. Searches of drains, abandoned structures, and stretches of the Los Angeles River turned up no decisive scene.

By spring of 1947, the Black Dahlia murder was sliding into cold-case status. Sergeant Finis Brown later blamed press interference for compromising the investigation. In September 1949, a grand jury examined the LAPD homicide unit’s failures in a string of unsolved killings of women and children, Elizabeth Short’s among them. Detectives retraced her path through Massachusetts, Florida, California, and even contacts in Texas and New Orleans. The interviews widened the biography. They did not produce a killer.

Around the same years, Los Angeles saw other brutal murders of women that papers tried to braid into one panic. Jeanne French was found beaten and nude in February 1947 with writing on her body that some read as a Black Dahlia taunt. Other cases filled the late forties. Links were suspected, argued, and never proven. Separately, writers and some officers wondered about a connection to Cleveland’s earlier Torso Murders. Original LAPD investigators studied that angle and discounted it. Decades later, detective John St. John looked at a man named Jack Anderson Wilson and believed he was close to an arrest before Wilson died in a fire in 1982. That trail also died with him.

No suspect has ever been officially confirmed. That has not stopped theories. In 1991, Janice Knowlton claimed she watched her father, George Knowlton, kill Elizabeth Short in a garage when she was a child. Excavation found no remains that proved it. St. John said her story did not match the facts. In 2003, retired LAPD detective Steve Hodel published Black Dahlia Avenger, accusing his own father, Dr. George Hill Hodel, a physician who lived in the distinctive Sowden House on Franklin Avenue. George Hodel had been secretly wiretapped for weeks in early 1950 during a district attorney investigation led by Lieutenant Frank Jemison. Notes later publicized include a recorded remark to the effect of, suppose I did kill the Black Dahlia, they could not prove it now. Steve Hodel treats that as confession. Critics note George Hodel was never charged with Elizabeth Short’s murder, and that meeting her was never proven to the satisfaction of a courtroom. In 2017, author Piu Eatwell argued for Leslie Dillon, a former mortician’s assistant turned bellhop, possibly acting in a web involving Mark Hansen and police corruption, with the Aster Motel as a possible crime scene. Other researchers, including Larry Harnisch, say district attorney files place Dillon in San Francisco when Short was killed, and that official denials undercut the motel story. The pattern is familiar. Strong suspicions. Conflicting files. No conviction.

Over the decades, more than five hundred people have confessed or been offered up as the killer, including people who were not even born in 1947. St. John once remarked how amazing it was how many people volunteer a relative. The confession machine became part of the legend, another fog bank over the evidence.

So did the myths about Elizabeth herself. She was cast as a promiscuous adventuress prowling Hollywood Boulevard. She was called a prostitute. She was said to have infantile genitalia that made sex impossible, a claim used both to sexualize and to erase her. Later review of grand jury and investigative material, as summarized by careful researchers, found no evidence she worked as a prostitute. District attorney and FBI files include statements from men who said they had sex with her. The autopsy notes a small uterus; it does not establish the popular congenital myth. The Hollywood starlet story was mostly costume. The woman who died was closer to a waitress with unstable housing than to a studio contract player.

What remains solid is narrower and colder. A twenty-two-year-old woman left the Biltmore orbit on January 9. Six days later her body appeared in a vacant lot, murdered elsewhere, cut apart with unsettling precision, washed, and displayed. Someone mailed her papers after bathing them in gasoline. Hundreds of officers chased leads across a city that wanted a monster and a newspaper that wanted a name. They got the name. They never got the man.

Elizabeth Short is buried at Mountain View Cemetery in Oakland. Phoebe eventually lived near that grave before returning east and dying in 1992. In February 1947, shock over the crime helped push California toward mandatory sex offender registration, one of the first such systems in the country. James Ellroy later turned the case into noir fiction. Hollywood filmed the fiction. Television borrowed the nickname for atmosphere. None of that closed the file.

If you strip away the Dahlia brand, what is left is a missing week, a vacant lot, a cleaned body, a gasoline-washed envelope, a mother lied to by a newspaper, and a city that still cannot say who killed her. That is the Black Dahlia case as the record leaves it: not a solved puzzle waiting for the right book, but an open wound in Los Angeles history, with Elizabeth Short at the center, and the killer still unnamed.
