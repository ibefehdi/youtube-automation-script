In the summer of 1985, Southern California learned to sleep with the lights on. Porch bulbs stayed burning until dawn. Gun shops ran out of inventory. Neighborhood watches formed on quiet streets that had never needed them. People who had lived for years with unlocked windows suddenly checked every latch twice. They were not afraid of a stranger on the freeway. They were afraid of someone who might already be inside the house.

Newspapers called him the Walk-In Killer. Then the Valley Intruder. Then the name that stuck: the Night Stalker. For months, no one knew who he was. What they did know was worse. He did not hunt one kind of victim. He entered homes at night and attacked men, women, children, and the elderly. Couples. People alone. Families who thought suburban walls meant safety. The fear was not only what he did. It was that he could choose anyone.

His name was Richard Ramirez. Born Ricardo Leyva Munoz Ramirez on February 29, 1960, in El Paso, Texas. The youngest of five children in a Mexican immigrant household. By the time California learned his face, he was a thin drifter with rotting teeth, a cocaine habit, and a long string of petty arrests. The public story would later swell with Satanic symbols and courtroom theatrics. The quieter truth underneath was simpler and colder. He broke into houses. He stole. He killed. And for more than a year, he kept getting away with it.

To understand how a city of millions ended up hunted by one man, you have to start before the nickname, before the mugshot, before the mob on an East Los Angeles street. You have to start with a childhood that biographies describe as violent and fractured, and with a cousin who filled a teenage mind with images of wartime cruelty. Those details matter as background. They do not excuse what came next. Nothing does.

In El Paso, according to later biographical accounts, Ramirez grew up under a father who drank and beat his children. He began using drugs young. An older cousin, Miguel Mike Valles, a Vietnam veteran, showed him Polaroid photographs and stories of sexual violence and killing overseas. On May 4, 1975, Ramirez was present when Valles fatally shot his own wife during an argument. Valles was later found not guilty by reason of insanity. Ramirez dropped out of high school, drifted into theft, and by 1982 had resettled in California. Cocaine became the center of gravity. Burglary paid for it. Then the burglaries stopped being only about money.

On April 10, 1984, in San Francisco's Tenderloin district, nine-year-old Mei Leung was murdered in the basement of the residential hotel where her family lived. For twenty-five years, that killing sat in a cold case file. In 2009, San Francisco police announced that DNA from the scene matched Richard Ramirez, who had been living in nearby Tenderloin hotels that spring. By then he was already on death row for other murders. He was never given a new completed trial for Mei Leung before he died. Years later, officials also disclosed a second DNA profile from that scene. Publicly, that second person was never charged. The case remains one of the darkest expansions of the Night Stalker story, and one of its most unfinished.

Two months after Mei Leung's death, on June 28, 1984, a different kind of silence broke in Glassell Park, Los Angeles. Jack Vincow came to check on his elderly mother, Jennie, seventy-nine years old. The screen was missing from an open living room window. The front door was unlocked. Inside, the apartment was in disorder. In the bedroom, he found his mother dead. She had been stabbed. Her throat had been cut so deeply that investigators and later court records described wounds almost from ear to ear. A fingerprint on the mesh screen would eventually help tie the scene to Ramirez. At the time, it was one terrible murder in a big city. It did not yet look like the opening chapter of a regional panic.

Then came the pause. Nine months of relative quiet in the public narrative. When the violence returned in March 1985, it returned with speed.

On March 17, in Rosemead, twenty-two-year-old Maria Hernandez pulled into her garage. A man shot her in the face with a twenty-two caliber handgun. She raised her hands. The bullet struck the keys she was holding and deflected. She played dead. Inside the house, her roommate, thirty-four-year-old Dayle Yoshie Okazaki, heard the shot. She ducked. When she lifted her head, the gunman was waiting. He shot her once in the forehead and killed her.

Within about an hour, in nearby Monterey Park, thirty-year-old Tsai-Lian Veronica Yu was pulled from her car and shot. She died. In a single night, Southern California had a double murder, a near miss, and a new predator in the headlines. Descriptions filtered out: thin, dark wavy hair, bulging eyes, widely spaced rotting teeth. The press reached for names. Walk-In Killer. Valley Intruder. Night Stalker.

One week later, outside Whittier, Vincent and Maxine Zazzara were killed in their home. Vincent was shot while sleeping. Maxine fought. She found a shotgun that was not loaded. The attacker shot her, then mutilated her body in ways that shocked even investigators hardened by homicide work. Outside the house, in the flower beds, police found shoe prints from an Avia athletic sneaker. Ballistics began to stitch scenes together. The same gun. The same pattern of night entry. A serial killer was no longer a theory. It was a working reality.

Through the spring and summer of 1985, the attacks kept coming, and the geography of dread widened across the San Gabriel Valley. In Monterey Park, Bill Doi was shot during a home invasion and later died. His disabled wife was bound and sexually assaulted. In Monrovia, elderly sisters Mabel Bell and Florence Lang were beaten in their home. Satanic symbols were drawn with lipstick. Both women later died from their injuries. In Burbank, Carol Kyle was bound with her young son and raped while valuables were stolen. In Arcadia, Mary Louise Cannon, a seventy-five-year-old grandmother, was murdered. In Sierra Madre, sixteen-year-old Whitney Bennett survived a tire iron attack and a strangulation attempt that left her needing hundreds of stitches. In Monterey Park again, Joyce Lucille Nelson was stomped to death. An Avia print was left on her face. Another woman, Sophie Dickman, was assaulted and robbed and ordered to swear on Satan that she was not hiding money.

Los Angeles County Sheriff Sherman Block and a growing multi-agency task force faced a problem that still confuses people who expect serial killers to be tidy. The methods varied. Guns. Knives. Bludgeoning. The victims varied. Detectives Frank Salerno and Gil Carrillo helped push the connection anyway, following ballistics and those rare Avia soles through a map that refused to stay still.

July twentieth was one of the worst nights. In Glendale, Lela and Maxon Kneiding were attacked with a machete and shot. In Sun Valley, Chainarong Khovananth was shot dead in his sleep. His wife Somkid was raped and forced to show where the family's valuables were kept while their child was bound. The attacker demanded she swear to Satan.

By August, the map of fear covered freeways and suburbs alike. Chris and Virginia Peterson were shot in Northridge and lived. In Diamond Bar, Elyas Abowath was shot dead. His wife Sakina was raped and ordered to swear on Satan while their small son was briefly tied. Then Ramirez moved north.

On August eighteenth, in San Francisco, Peter Pan was shot and killed. Barbara Pan was beaten, sexually assaulted, and shot. She survived. On a wall, lipstick left a pentagram and the words Jack the Knife. Again, an Avia shoe print. Detectives in Los Angeles and San Francisco could now see the same feet, the same guns, the same night logic moving up the coast.

Then came a mistake that was not the killer's. San Francisco Mayor Dianne Feinstein discussed investigative details in a public press conference, including information about the shoe evidence. Detectives were furious. Case literature later held that Ramirez, who followed his own coverage, discarded the Avia shoes, with accounts saying he threw them from the Golden Gate Bridge. Whether every detail of that disposal can be proven, the operational wound was real. A key forensic trail had been televised to the man it was meant to catch.

But the Night Stalker made a different mistake in Orange County.

On August twenty-fourth, 1985, in Mission Viejo, thirteen-year-old James Romero the Third noticed a prowler near his family's home. He noted an orange Toyota station wagon and part of a license plate. The same night, nearby, thirty-year-old Bill Carns was shot in the head three times. He survived after surgeons removed two of the bullets. His fiancée, twenty-nine-year-old Inez Erickson, was raped. The attacker told her he was the Night Stalker. Before leaving, he told her to tell them he had been there.

Days later, the stolen orange Toyota was found abandoned in Los Angeles. Orange County criminalists spent hours over the car with an argon ion laser, coaxing fingerprints that ordinary powder might have missed. Those prints went into a computerized system that was still new. It returned a name: Richard Ramirez, twenty-five, a Texas-born Los Angeles drifter with a record for auto theft and other lesser crimes. On December twelfth, 1984, he had been booked for a stolen vehicle. That mugshot was about to become the most important photograph in California.

On August thirtieth and thirty-first, authorities released his face. The message from law enforcement was blunt. They knew who he was. Soon everyone else would. There would be nowhere to hide.

Ramirez did not know. He had taken a bus to Tucson to see his brother, then come back. Early on August thirty-first, he walked into the ordinary morning of East Los Angeles. At a store, he saw elderly women staring. He saw his own face on a newspaper rack. In Spanish-language coverage he was the night invader. In English he was the Night Stalker. Either way, the anonymity that had protected him was gone.

He ran. He crossed lanes of the Santa Ana Freeway on foot. He tried to take cars. Residents fought back. Faustino Pinon. Jose Burgoin. Angelina De La Torre, whose keys he tried to seize. Her husband Manuel struck him with a fence post. A growing crowd chased him down Hubbard Street in Boyle Heights and beat him until police arrived. The serial killer who had terrorized half the state was stopped not by a SWAT raid, but by neighbors who recognized a newspaper face and refused to let him leave.

Fingerprints confirmed it. The man in custody was Richard Ramirez.

What followed was one of the most expensive and theatrical murder trials California had seen, with costs later reported around one point six to one point eight million dollars. Jury selection began in July 1988. Early in the proceedings, Ramirez raised a hand marked with a pentagram and shouted hail Satan. The media drank it in. Prosecutors P. Philip Halpin and Alan Yochelson built a quieter case: fingerprints, shoe prints, ballistics, and survivors who looked at him in court and said he was the man. They called more than a hundred witnesses. Six women who had survived attacks identified him.

In August 1988, juror Phyllis Singletary was found shot to death in her apartment. Terror moved through the jury box. People wondered if Ramirez could reach them from a cell. Investigators determined her boyfriend had killed her, then died by suicide with the same weapon. The fear was real. The conspiracy was not. Deliberations had to restart with alternates.

On September twentieth, 1989, after roughly twenty-two days of deliberation, a jury of seven women and five men convicted Ramirez of all forty-three counts. Twelve first-degree murders. One second-degree murder, for Tsai-Lian Yu. Five attempted murders. Eleven sexual assault counts across rape, sodomy, and oral copulation charges. Fourteen first-degree burglaries. Thirteen people murdered in the Los Angeles case that went to verdict. About twenty-five victims across the charged attacks, including survivors who had to live with the memory.

In November 1989, Superior Court Judge Michael Tynan sentenced him to death. Relatives of the murdered and survivors spoke of pain that did not end when the verdicts were read. Ramirez told the court he would be avenged. To reporters he offered a shrug that became infamous: big deal, death always went with the territory, see you in Disneyland. The line was pure performance. The families were left with funerals, trauma, and years of court dates.

California's Supreme Court affirmed the convictions and death sentence in 2006. Further appeals dragged on. California's death penalty system was tangled in litigation. Ramirez was never executed. On June seventh, 2013, at age fifty-three, he died at Marin General Hospital of complications from B-cell lymphoma. The coroner also listed chronic substance abuse and hepatitis C as significant conditions. He had spent nearly twenty-four years on death row at San Quentin. His body, according to reports, went unclaimed and was cremated.

In the years between sentence and death, the myth of the Night Stalker grew in ways that had little to do with justice. Correspondents wrote to him. In 1996 he married Doreen Lioy inside San Quentin. True crime culture turned pentagrams into aesthetic. The victims' names were harder to find than his. When Bill Carns spoke after Ramirez died, the tone from survivors was not celebration of a legend. It was the complicated relief of a chapter finally closed by biology instead of the gas chamber.

That is the distortion this story has to correct.

The Night Stalker case is not mainly about a courtroom devil pose. It is about Jennie Vincow's son opening an unlocked door. It is about Maria Hernandez surviving because a set of keys caught a bullet. It is about a teenager writing down a partial license plate. It is about criminalists bent over a stolen Toyota with a laser. It is about East Los Angeles residents deciding, in broad daylight, that a killer would not take another car and disappear.

It is also about what remains unfinished. How many crimes beyond the forty-three Los Angeles counts. What exactly happened in the Tenderloin basement in 1984, and who else the DNA might suggest was there. Which cold files from that era still deserve another look. How a region remembers a summer when safety felt like a rumor.

There is a second naming tangle that matters too. Before Ramirez, another California series had been called the Night Stalker. That offender was later identified as Joseph James DeAngelo, the Golden State Killer, and retrospectively labeled the Original Night Stalker. Ramirez's fame swallowed the nickname. History had to pry the labels apart again. Even the title of the terror was contested.

When people ask how the Night Stalker was caught, the honest answer is almost disappointingly human. Not a psychic. Not a last-second confession. A shoe. A print. A plate number. A computer that had only recently learned how to search fingerprints at scale. A photograph on cheap newsprint. And a street full of people who were done being afraid.

Southern California locked its doors in 1985 because one man taught an entire region that night could walk inside. The doors eventually opened again. The names of the dead did not come back. That is the part the mugshot never showed. That is the part worth remembering when the legend tries to make a monster look like a myth.
