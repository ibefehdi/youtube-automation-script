The phone in Room 1046 kept slipping off the hook. Each time the hotel switchboard lit up, someone had to go back to the tenth floor, back to the same locked door, back to the same room where a guest using the name Roland T. Owen had spent two days sitting in the dark and waiting for someone named Don. When a bellboy finally got inside on the morning of January 4, 1935, he found a scene that made no sense at all. The man in the room was still alive, badly injured, and yet almost everything that might have explained what happened was gone.

To understand why this case still unsettles people nearly a century later, you have to begin with a hotel that still stands in downtown Kansas City. In 1935, the Hotel President sat at 14th and Baltimore in a city known for nightlife, corruption, and the easy anonymity of the Pendergast era. Travelers came and went. False names were not unheard of. But even by those standards, the young man who checked in on January 2 left an impression. At about 1:20 that afternoon, he registered as Roland T. Owen, gave a Los Angeles address, paid for one night, and asked for an interior room several floors up. Bellboy Randolph Propst escorted him to Room 1046, a small room facing the courtyard rather than the street. Privacy seemed to matter to him.

Propst later said the guest was neatly dressed in a dark overcoat and carried no luggage. He mentioned staying at the Muehlebach the night before, but said the five-dollar room rate there was too high. In Room 1046, he took only three things from his coat pocket and set them out: a comb, a hairbrush, and toothpaste. That was it. No suitcase. No extra clothes. Nothing to suggest he planned to remain in Kansas City, and nothing to explain why a man traveling under an assumed name wanted a room tucked away from public view. From the beginning, there was a sense that he was not simply checking in. He was taking cover.

The person who saw him most clearly over the next two days was hotel maid Mary Soptic. She found the room dark, with the shades drawn and only dim light inside. Later she said he seemed worried, or afraid, and that he always wanted to keep in the dark. At one point he asked her to leave the door unlocked because he was expecting a friend. When Soptic returned later that afternoon, she found him lying across the bed, fully dressed, and on a table or desk nearby was the clue that would define the entire case. It read, "Don, I will be back in fifteen minutes. Wait."

On the morning of January 3, Soptic used her passkey to enter Room 1046 and found Owen inside, even though the door had been locked from the outside. While she cleaned, she overheard him on the telephone saying, "No, Don, I don't want to eat. I am not hungry. I just had breakfast." Soptic also said he asked whether she covered the whole floor and whether the hotel was residential, as if he wanted to know exactly who might be nearby.

Later that same day, around four in the afternoon, Soptic returned with fresh towels. This time she heard two male voices inside the room. When she knocked, a deep rough voice answered, "We don't need any." It stood out to her because she had removed the room's towels earlier. There should have been none inside. If someone said no towels were needed, then someone else was in that room, someone who either did not want staff coming in or did not want questions about what was already happening behind the door. Soptic did not believe the voice was Owen's.

Other fragments from that night only make the picture stranger. A guest named Jean Owen, staying in Room 1048, later said she heard loud talking and cursing on the floor overnight, and that there were both male and female voices. Her boyfriend, Joe Reinert of the Midland Flower Shop, had been visiting her, placing at least one more outside witness near the scene. Elevator operator Charles Blocher described a woman known around the hotel as a frequent visitor, sometimes described in period language as a commercial woman, asking about Room 1026, not 1046, before moving through the hotel with a man. Later writers have warned that a wire-service error may have confused those room numbers, so her connection to Room 1046 remains uncertain. The possibility is there, but it cannot be treated as proven.

Then there is Robert Lane, a water department employee whose story became one of the case's strangest side paths. Around eleven that night, near 13th and Lydia, Lane said a man in an undershirt, pants, and shoes flagged him down. The man had a scratch on his arm and seemed to be holding his hands in a way that may have been hiding blood. According to Lane, the man said he would kill someone tomorrow. Later Lane believed the man was the victim from Room 1046, but Detective Ira Johnson reportedly doubted that identification.

By the following morning, the switchboard at the Hotel President kept signaling trouble. The telephone in Room 1046 was off the hook again and again. Around seven o'clock, Randolph Propst went up to check. A "Do Not Disturb" sign hung on the door. He knocked. From inside came a deep voice telling him to come in and then to turn on the lights. But the door was locked, and Propst could not enter. He told the occupant to put the phone back on the receiver and left. At about 8:30, another bellboy, Harold Pike, entered with a passkey. He saw the guest naked on the bed with dark stains on the sheets, assumed he was drunk, put the telephone back in place, and left. Only later, around 10:30 or 11 that morning, when the phone was again reported off the hook and Propst returned, did the truth become impossible to miss. He found the room bloodied and the guest gravely wounded. When staff tried to re-enter, the door would open only a few inches because the man's body had fallen against it.

What happened inside Room 1046 was not ambiguous in the way some legends later made it sound. Assistant manager M. S. Weaver, other hotel staff including Percy Tyrrell, detectives Ira Johnson, William Eldredge, Sergeant Frank Howland, later Detective D. C. King, and Dr. Harold F. Flanders from General Hospital all became part of the immediate response. The injured man had cord tied around his neck, wrists, and ankles. There was bruising that suggested an attempted strangulation. He had multiple stab wounds to the chest, including one that punctured a lung. He had a fracture on the right side of his skull. Blood was on the bed, on the walls, in the bathroom, and even on the ceiling. Flanders estimated the injuries had likely been inflicted six to seven hours earlier, often translated in later retellings to around four or five in the morning. Detective Johnson quickly rejected the idea of suicide, telling reporters there was no doubt someone else was mixed up in it.

Even the victim's own reported words, famous as they are, have to be handled carefully. Later newspaper accounts said that when he was asked who hurt him, he answered, "Nobody." They also claimed he said he had fallen against the bathtub and denied trying to kill himself. Those lines became central to the mythology of Room 1046, as if the dying man was still protecting someone. But a much later review of the surviving medical affidavit by Kansas City Magazine suggested the original paperwork may not record him speaking at all. So those final words belong in the story, because they were reported and widely repeated, but they do not belong there as undisputed fact. What is not disputed is that he slipped into a coma and died shortly after midnight on January 5, 1935.

The room itself offered investigators almost as much information through absence as through evidence. There were no clothes, no luggage, no towels, no soap or hotel toiletries, and no knife. The likely weapon was missing. What police did find felt like the remains of a scene that had been deliberately stripped down: a necktie label from Botany Worsted Mills of Passaic, New Jersey, a hairpin, a safety pin, an unsmoked cigarette, an unused bottle of dilute sulfuric acid, and two drinking glasses, one of them broken in the sink. Four small fingerprints on the telephone stand were thought possibly to belong to a woman, but they were never matched.

That mystery deepened almost immediately because Roland T. Owen did not seem to exist. Detectives began tracing the Los Angeles address from the hotel register and found nothing useful. As they widened the search, more names began to surface. The nearby Muehlebach Hotel had apparently known the same man as Eugene K. Scott of Los Angeles. The St. Regis Hotel had a record for Duncan Ogletree sharing a room with a man named Donald Kelso. That connection mattered because Don, whoever he was, was already sitting at the center of the timeline. Then came another possible identification. Wrestling promoter Toni Bernardi thought he recognized the victim as a man who called himself Cecil Werner of Omaha, though Omaha promoter Charles Loch did not confirm it. An even more dramatic claim collapsed outright when a man named Ernest Johnson identified the victim as his cousin Harvey, only for the family to prove that Harvey had died years earlier. It was as if the victim kept resolving into focus and then disappearing again.

For months, the dead man in Room 1046 remained exactly that: a dead man without a true name. Police circulated fingerprints and photographs. Newspapers and radio accounts spread the story far beyond Kansas City. Thousands reportedly viewed the body at the funeral home, yet nobody identified him. The mystery had all the elements that drew public fascination in 1935: a handsome young traveler, a false name, a locked room, possible underworld connections, and just enough clues to invite certainty without ever rewarding it. But behind the public fascination was a person whose family had no idea where he was, what name he was using, or that he had already been killed.

The next act of the story is one of the strangest. In March 1935, when burial in a potter's field was being arranged for the unidentified victim, an anonymous man telephoned and asked that the funeral be delayed. In some published versions of the call, he said the dead man would be buried near his sister. He also reportedly said that cheaters usually get what is coming to them. Then, on March 23, twenty-five dollars wrapped in newspaper was sent to Mellody-McGilley funeral home. A florist received instructions and payment arranged through pay phones, and soon 13 American Beauty roses were delivered with a card signed, "Love for ever - Louise," in disguised handwriting. Reverend E. B. Shively of Roanoke Christian Church officiated. Police attended. A stakeout was set at the grave, and nothing came of it. Another woman later called the Journal-Post to say the newspaper had it wrong and that "He got into a jam." The burial went forward at Memorial Park Cemetery in Kansas City, Kansas.

The dead man would finally get his name back because of his mother. Ruby Ogletree lived in Birmingham, Alabama, though Artemus had been born in Florida and was one of three children. In the fall of 1936, a friend showed her a May 1935 issue of American Weekly featuring the story of the unidentified victim in Room 1046. She recognized something almost no stranger could have known for certain: a large horizontal scar on the scalp above one ear, left from a childhood accident involving hot grease, an area where hair never grew back. That scar was the key. Ruby exchanged letters and photographs with Kansas City police, and on November 2, 1936, the victim was publicly identified as her son, Artemus Ogletree.

Even his age is a point where the record has to be treated with care. Ruby said Artemus left home in April 1934 at age 17, hitchhiking toward California. The family wired him money, and he sent handwritten letters along the way. Many modern retellings describe him as 19 at the time of his death. Hotel staff in 1935 guessed that Roland T. Owen looked anywhere from about 20 to 35. So the honest version is not a neat one. Artemus was very young, but the exact age attached to the final Kansas City story depends on which source you trust. The uncertainty does not change the human reality. A mother had been searching for her son while the country knew him only as a mystery.

And Ruby's ordeal did not end with identification. After Artemus had died in Kansas City, she received typewritten letters from Chicago and New York implying that he was alive. Then, in August 1935, a man calling from Memphis used the name Godfrey Jordan and claimed Artemus was alive, married in Cairo, and missing a thumb after a fight. Ruby wrote to federal authorities, contacted the Cairo consul, and searched for records that might confirm it. None did. Ruby later came to suspect that the caller might actually have been Joe Simpson, the friend who had left home with Artemus, but that suspicion was never proved.

Once Artemus was identified, the name Donald Kelso became even harder to ignore. It already appeared in the St. Regis records alongside the alias Duncan Ogletree. Years later, in 1937, New York police arrested a man known as Joseph Martin, also called Joseph Ogden and Donald Kelso, for the murder of a roommate whose body had been shipped in a trunk toward Memphis. That case made journalists and investigators look back at Room 1046 with renewed intensity. The New Yorker later reported that Kansas City police had linked his handwriting to the letters sent to Ruby. But later FBI review complicated that picture by concluding that the Donald Kelso signature on the hotel record was not written by Joseph Ogden. In other words, the cleanest version of the suspect theory did not hold. No charges were ever filed in the Ogletree case.

That is why this murder resists summary. Every lead has weight, and then almost every lead breaks under closer pressure. Don might have been Donald Kelso, or might not. Joseph Ogden might have been involved, or might simply have passed through the story because his aliases overlapped with it. The woman described by the elevator operator might have been crucial, or might belong to an entirely different room because of a reporting error. Robert Lane might have encountered Artemus himself, or a participant, or no one connected to the case at all. Even the famous bedside line, "Nobody," may be more a product of newspaper repetition than surviving official record.

And yet some things are clear. Artemus Ogletree checked into the Hotel President on January 2, 1935, under the name Roland T. Owen. He had also used Eugene K. Scott, and at another point Duncan Ogletree. He may have been known somewhere else as Cecil Werner. He wanted interior rooms. He carried almost nothing. He kept the shades drawn. He told hotel staff he expected a friend. A note to Don was left behind. A call to Don was overheard. A second male voice answered the maid's knock. Sometime before dawn on January 4, he was bound, beaten, and stabbed in Room 1046. The weapon and much of the room's movable evidence disappeared. He died without giving investigators a clear path to whoever had been with him. Those facts are not rumor. They are the hard frame around everything else.

The case remained open for years. Detectives reviewed it into the 1950s, but no definitive answer emerged. In 2012, researcher John Arthur Horner brought renewed attention to the case through his detailed retelling and disclosed one more strange footnote. Around 2003, he received an out-of-state phone call from someone who claimed that after a person's death they had found a shoebox of clippings about the Room 1046 case, along with an unnamed item mentioned in old newspaper coverage. The caller would not identify themselves, and they would not name the item. The Hotel President still stands today as the Hilton President. Room 1046 became part of legend. Artemus Ogletree should be remembered as more than that.

So the case remains where it began, with a few details that refuse to loosen their grip. Why did he ask for an interior room and insist on darkness? Who was Don, really? Why were there no clothes, no towels, no weapon, and no clear trail out of Room 1046? Who sent the money, the roses, and the card from Louise? Why tell Ruby that her son was alive in Cairo when he had already been buried in Kansas? And if someone cared enough to protect his burial, why did no one care enough to tell the truth?

There is no satisfying final reveal here. No courtroom answer. No confession. No recovered weapon. Only a locked room that was not truly locked, an identity that arrived too late, and a chain of names that never quite resolves into a single face. Artemus Ogletree left home as a very young man, whether 17 as his mother said when he departed or closer to 19 by later retellings, and somewhere between Birmingham, California, and Kansas City, he vanished into his aliases. What remains is not closure, but a room, a note, a telephone off the hook, and the quiet fact that someone walked away and was never made to answer for it.
