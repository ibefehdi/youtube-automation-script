### VIDEO TITLE

The Unsolved Murder in Room 1046

### VIDEO TOPIC / CASE NAME

The 1935 Hotel President / Room 1046 murder of Artemus Ogletree, who checked in under the alias Roland T. Owen, Kansas City, Missouri. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)

### ONE-SENTENCE LOGLINE

In January 1935, a young traveler using the name Roland T. Owen checked into Kansas City's Hotel President with no luggage, kept his room dark, waited for someone named Don, was found fatally assaulted in Room 1046, and even after his real identity as Artemus Ogletree was uncovered, the case remained unsolved. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)

### WHY THIS STORY WORKS

- It begins with a perfect documentary hook: a neatly dressed young man arrives alone, asks for an interior room, unpacks only a comb, hairbrush, and toothpaste, and spends the next two days acting as if he is waiting for someone he fears. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; A&E 2025)
- The mystery is layered rather than one-note. There is the false name, the darkened room, the note to "Don," the missing clothes and towels, the strange funeral benefactors, and the later discovery that the victim's mother received letters after her son was already dead. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; AL.com 2018)
- The case is visually rich without needing graphic depiction: the grand downtown hotel, a small interior room with drawn shades, switchboard calls, a bellboy at a locked door, newspaper front pages, a funeral bouquet signed "Love for ever - Louise," and a family in Alabama trying to understand impossible letters. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The victim deserves a careful, humane telling. Artemus Ogletree was not just "the man in Room 1046." He was a son from Florida and Birmingham, one of three children, a young traveler whose mother kept searching for him long after others had given up. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- The story remains unresolved, which gives the ending weight. There are suspects, aliases, suspicious callers, and later investigative theories, but no definitive answer and no one ever charged. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021; WDAF/FOX4)

### CONFIRMED FACTS

- Artemus Ogletree was born in Florida around 1915 and was one of three children. His family later lived in Birmingham, Alabama. A childhood accident involving hot grease left a large horizontal scar on his scalp above one ear, and the area remained hairless; that scar later became the key to identifying him. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- Ruby Ogletree said Artemus left home in April 1934 at age 17, hitchhiking toward California. The family wired him money, and he sent handwritten updates. Modern retellings often describe him as 19 at death, while hotel staff estimates for "Owen" ranged from about 20 to 35, so his exact age in retellings must be handled as a conflict rather than a settled fact. (Source: AL.com 2018; A&E 2025; Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- At about 1:20 p.m. on January 2, 1935, he checked into the Hotel President at 14th and Baltimore in Kansas City, Missouri, using the name Roland T. Owen and a Los Angeles address. He paid for one night and asked for an interior room several floors up. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025; Wikipedia pointer)
- Bellboy Randolph Propst escorted him to Room 1046, an interior room overlooking the courtyard. Propst later said the guest was neatly dressed in a dark overcoat, had no luggage, and mentioned that he had stayed at the Muehlebach the night before but found the $5 room rate too high. The only items he unpacked were a hairbrush, a comb, and toothpaste from his overcoat pocket. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Hotel maid Mary Soptic encountered him several times. She found the room dark with the shades drawn and said he appeared worried or afraid and "always wanted to kinda keep in the dark." He asked that the room be left unlocked because he was expecting a friend. Later that afternoon she found him dressed on the bed, and on the desk or table was a note reading, "Don, I will be back in fifteen minutes. Wait." (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- On the morning of January 3, Soptic opened the room with her passkey and found him inside even though the door had been locked from the outside. While she worked, she overheard him on the phone saying, "No, Don, I don't want to eat. I am not hungry. I just had breakfast." He also asked whether she covered the whole floor and whether the hotel was residential. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- At about 4 p.m. on January 3, Soptic returned with towels, heard two men inside, and a rough, deep voice, not believed to be Owen's, told her, "We don't need any." That stood out because she knew there were no towels in the room; she had removed them earlier. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- Another guest, Jean Owen of Lee's Summit, stayed in Room 1048 and said she heard loud talking and cursing, with male and female voices, on the floor overnight while her boyfriend Joe Reinert visited from the Midland Flower Shop. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- Elevator operator Charles Blocher reported a woman known to staff as a frequent visitor or "commercial woman" asking about Room 1026, not 1046, and later moving around the hotel with a man. Modern writers note that a wire-service error may have confused 1026 and 1046, so her connection to Artemus Ogletree is uncertain, not proven. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Water department employee Robert Lane said that around 11 p.m. on January 3, near 13th and Lydia, a man in an undershirt, pants, and shoes flagged him down, had a scratch on his arm, seemed to be cupping his hands to hide possible blood, and said he would kill someone "tomorrow." Lane later believed the man was Ogletree, but Detective Ira Johnson reportedly doubted the identification. (Source: Mental Floss; Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- On the morning of January 4, the telephone in Room 1046 was repeatedly found off the hook. Propst first went up around 7 a.m., saw a "Do Not Disturb" sign, heard a deep voice tell him to come in and then to turn on the lights, but could not enter because the door was locked. Around 8:30 a.m., Harold Pike let himself in with a passkey, saw the guest naked on the bed with dark stains on the sheets, assumed he was drunk, reset the phone, and left. Around 10:30 or 11 a.m., Propst returned, entered, and found Ogletree wounded, with blood on his head and blood in the room and bathroom. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Assistant manager M.S. Weaver, hotel staff including Percy Tyrrell, detectives Ira Johnson, William Eldredge, Sgt. Frank Howland, and later D.C. King responded, along with Dr. Harold F. Flanders of General Hospital. When staff tried to re-enter, the door could open only about six inches because Ogletree's body had fallen against it. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts based on KCPD file; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Ogletree had cord tied around his neck, wrists, and ankles; there was bruising suggesting an attempted strangulation; he had multiple stab wounds to the chest, with one puncturing a lung; and he suffered a skull fracture on the right side. Blood was found on the bed, walls, bathroom, and even on the ceiling. Flanders estimated the wounds had likely been inflicted about six to seven hours earlier, often rendered in later summaries as roughly 4 to 5 a.m. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; A&E 2025; Wikipedia pointer)
- Newspapers later reported that when asked who hurt him he answered "Nobody," claimed he fell against the bathtub, and denied trying to kill himself. Kansas City Magazine's 2021 review of the original medical affidavit noted that the affidavit may not record the victim speaking at all, so those lines should be treated as reported in later press, but disputed in the surviving paperwork. (Source: San Francisco Examiner 1935 as cited by A&E; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- He died shortly after midnight on January 5, 1935, at the hospital, after slipping into a coma. Detective Johnson told reporters, "There is no doubt that someone else is mixed up in this." (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- The room was remarkable for what was missing. Investigators found no clothing, no towels, no soap or hotel toiletries, and no knife. What they did find included a necktie label from Botany Worsted Mills of Passaic, New Jersey, a hairpin, a safety pin, an unsmoked cigarette, an unused bottle of dilute sulfuric acid, and two drinking glasses, one broken in the sink. Four small fingerprints on the phone stand were thought possibly to be a woman's, but they were never matched. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Detectives soon concluded that Roland T. Owen was an alias. The nearby Muehlebach Hotel knew him as Eugene K. Scott of Los Angeles, and the St. Regis Hotel had records for Duncan Ogletree sharing a room with Donald Kelso, a possible "Don." Wrestling promoter Toni Bernardi thought he recognized the victim as a man calling himself Cecil Werner of Omaha, but Omaha promoter Charles Loch did not confirm that identification. Another apparent identification, by Ernest Johnson as his cousin Harvey, collapsed when the family proved the cousin had died years earlier. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- In March 1935, when burial in potter's field was announced, an anonymous male caller asked that the burial be delayed, said cheaters get what is coming to them, and claimed the dead man would be buried near his sister. On March 23, $25 wrapped in newspaper was sent to Mellody-McGilley. A florist received payment requests and later sent 13 American Beauty roses with a card reading "Love for ever - Louise" in disguised handwriting. Reverend E.B. Shively of Roanoke Christian Church officiated. Police attended, and a stakeout of the grave produced nothing. A woman also called the Journal-Post to say the paper was wrong and that "He got into a jam." Ogletree was buried at Memorial Park Cemetery in Kansas City, Kansas. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer; user-provided confirmed facts)
- In the fall of 1936, a friend showed Ruby Ogletree the May 1935 American Weekly story "The Mystery of Room No. 1046," and she recognized her son from the scar photo. On November 2, 1936, after exchanging letters and photographs with Kansas City police, the victim was publicly identified as Artemus Ogletree. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; AL.com 2018; American Weekly May 1935)
- Ruby had already received typewritten letters after her son's death from Chicago and New York, and in August 1935 a caller from Memphis using the name Godfrey Jordan claimed Artemus was alive, married in Cairo, and missing a thumb after a fight. Ruby suspected the caller may actually have been Joe Simpson, the friend who left home with Artemus. She even wrote to federal authorities and the Cairo consul, but no steamship or consular records confirmed an Egypt trip. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; AL.com 2018; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- In 1937, New York police arrested Joseph Martin, also known as Joseph Ogden and Donald Kelso, for the murder of a roommate whose body was shipped in a trunk toward Memphis. The New Yorker later stated Kansas City police linked his handwriting to the letters sent to Ruby, but later FBI review said the signature of Donald Kelso on the hotel record was not written by Joseph Ogden. No charges were filed in the Ogletree case. Detectives reviewed the case into the 1950s, and it remains unsolved. (Source: The New Yorker Jan. 8, 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- John Arthur Horner disclosed in 2012 that around 2003 he received an out-of-state call from someone who said they had found a shoebox of clippings about the case and an unnamed item mentioned in newspaper coverage among a deceased person's belongings. The caller would not identify the item or themselves. The Hotel President still exists today as the Hilton President. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; WDAF/FOX4; Wikipedia pointer)

- Around 1915: Artemus Ogletree is born in Florida. Later family life centers in Birmingham, Alabama. (Source: AL.com 2018; Wikipedia pointer)

- April 1934: Ruby Ogletree says Artemus leaves home, age 17 by her account, hitchhiking west toward California. The family wires him money. He sends handwritten letters updating them. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2)
- Mid-1934 to late 1934: Artemus and Joe Simpson travel west; later accounts place Artemus in Los Angeles and then in Kansas City by August 1934. At some point he stays at the St. Regis in Kansas City as Duncan Ogletree with Donald Kelso. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Horner Pt. 2; Wikipedia pointer)
- Late 1934: Wrestling promoter Toni Bernardi says a man he knew as Cecil Werner approached him about wrestling matches. This possible sighting is never firmly tied down. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- January 1, 1935: He reportedly stays at the Muehlebach Hotel under the name Eugene K. Scott of Los Angeles. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- January 2, 1935, about 1:20 p.m.: He checks into the Hotel President as Roland T. Owen, asks for an interior room several floors up, pays for one night, and is escorted by Randolph Propst to Room 1046. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025)
- January 2, 1935, shortly after check-in: Propst sees him unpack only a hairbrush, comb, and toothpaste from his overcoat. He leaves the hotel after check-in. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025)
- January 2, 1935, afternoon: Maid Mary Soptic finds the room dark, with shades drawn and one dim light. Ogletree appears nervous or afraid, asks that the door be left unlocked because he is expecting a friend, and later a note appears reading, "Don, I will be back in fifteen minutes. Wait." (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- January 3, 1935, around 10:30 a.m.: Soptic finds the room locked from the outside but the guest inside. She hears him tell "Don" on the phone that he is not hungry and has already had breakfast. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- January 3, 1935, around 4 p.m.: Soptic hears two men in the room and a deep voice tells her, "We don't need any" towels. (Source: Horner Pt. 1)
- January 3, 1935, evening and night: Jean Owen in Room 1048 and others hear loud voices and cursing on the floor. Charles Blocher's elevator log of sorts places a known female visitor and at least one man moving between floors, though the exact room link remains uncertain. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- January 3, 1935, around 11 p.m.: Robert Lane says a lightly clothed man with a scratched arm flags him down near 13th and Lydia and says he will kill someone tomorrow. Lane later believes the man was Ogletree, though police are skeptical. (Source: Mental Floss; Horner Pt. 1)
- January 4, 1935, about 7 a.m.: The switchboard shows Room 1046's phone off the hook. Propst goes up, finds the door locked and a "Do Not Disturb" sign hanging, hears a deep voice, and leaves after telling the occupant to hang up the phone. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- January 4, 1935, about 8:30 a.m.: Harold Pike enters with a passkey, sees the guest naked on the bed with dark marks on the sheets, assumes he is drunk, and resets the phone. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- January 4, 1935, roughly 10:30 to 11 a.m.: Propst returns when the phone is again off the hook, enters, and finds Ogletree wounded and the room bloodied. Hotel staff and police are summoned. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- January 4, 1935, late morning: Dr. Harold F. Flanders and detectives document cords at the neck, wrists, and ankles, chest stab wounds, a punctured lung, skull fractures, and dried blood suggesting the assault happened hours earlier. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- January 5, 1935, shortly after midnight: Artemus Ogletree, still officially unknown, dies at the hospital after slipping into a coma. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025)
- January 1935: Police determine Roland T. Owen and Eugene K. Scott do not exist in Los Angeles as given. Publicity spreads nationwide. Thousands reportedly view the body at the funeral home, but nobody identifies him. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Horner Pt. 1)
- March 3-4, 1935: Planned burial in potter's field is interrupted by anonymous calls promising to pay for a proper funeral and burial. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- March 23, 1935: Cash wrapped in newspaper reaches Mellody-McGilley; flowers signed "Love for ever - Louise" are ordered and paid for through pay-phone arrangements. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Wikipedia pointer)
- Spring to August 1935: Ruby Ogletree receives suspicious typewritten letters and then a Memphis call from "Godfrey Jordan," all implying Artemus is alive abroad. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018)
- May 1935: American Weekly publishes "The Mystery of Room No. 1046" with a profile image that later helps Ruby identify him. (Source: American Weekly May 1935; Horner Pt. 2)
- November 2, 1936: Kansas City police publicly confirm that the victim was Artemus Ogletree after Ruby provides letters, photos, and the identifying scar history. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018)
- 1937: Joseph Martin / Joseph Ogden / Donald Kelso is arrested in New York in another murder case involving a trunk shipped toward Memphis. His aliases and the similarity of method attract Kansas City's attention, but no Ogletree charges follow. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Late 1930s to 1950s: The Ogletree file stays open and is reviewed periodically, but no conclusive new evidence appears. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Around 2003: John Arthur Horner receives the mysterious shoebox call about clippings and an unnamed item linked to the case. (Source: Horner Pt. 2)
- 2012: Horner publishes his two-part Kansas City Public Library retelling, reviving public interest. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2)
- 2021 to 2025: Kansas City Magazine, WDAF/FOX4, A&E, and other modern outlets continue to revisit the case, but it remains open and unsolved. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; WDAF/FOX4; A&E 2025)

### PEOPLE

- **Artemus Ogletree / Roland T. Owen / Eugene K. Scott / Duncan Ogletree / possibly Cecil Werner**: Victim. Young traveler from Florida and Birmingham whose real name was not publicly known for nearly two years. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- **Ruby Ogletree**: Artemus's mother. Central to the eventual identification. She preserved his letters, recognized the scar from American Weekly, and kept pushing authorities after receiving impossible typewritten letters and the Memphis call. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Mary Soptic**: Hotel maid whose observations provide the clearest portrait of Artemus's final two days alive: the darkness, the fear, the request to leave the door unlocked, the note to Don, and the deep second voice. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Randolph Propst**: Bellboy who escorted Ogletree to Room 1046 and later became one of the first people to witness the aftermath. His recollections frame both the arrival and the discovery. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025)
- **Harold Pike**: Bellboy who entered at about 8:30 a.m. on January 4, saw the guest naked on the bed, assumed he was drunk, and left after resetting the phone. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Jean Owen**: Unrelated guest in Room 1048 who reported hearing men and women loudly talking and cursing on the floor during the crucial night. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- **Joe Reinert**: Jean Owen's boyfriend, associated with Midland Flower Shop, who visited her that night and corroborated her presence. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts; Wikipedia pointer)
- **Charles Blocher**: Elevator operator whose account introduced the mysterious female visitor and another man, though the room-number confusion leaves that lead uncertain. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Robert Lane**: Water department employee who may have encountered either Artemus or a related participant on the night of January 3. His testimony is striking but contested. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Detective Ira Johnson**: Kansas City detective who publicly rejected suicide and stated that someone else was involved. He also reportedly doubted Lane's identification. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- **William Eldredge, Sgt. Frank Howland, D.C. King**: Other investigators named in surviving retellings and file-based summaries. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts based on KCPD file)
- **Dr. Harold F. Flanders**: Physician from General Hospital who examined Ogletree, cut the cords, described the injuries, and estimated they were hours old by the time he arrived. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Donald Kelso / Don**: The most persistent shadow in the case. The name appears in the note, the phone call, and St. Regis records. Whether Don was protector, companion, handler, or killer remains unknown. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- **Joseph Martin / Joseph Ogden**: New York murder suspect later associated with the alias Donald Kelso. The New Yorker and Kansas City police interest made him the most famous later suspect, but the connection did not hold up strongly enough for charges. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Joe Simpson**: Artemus's friend from Alabama who left home with him. Ruby later suspected he was the voice from Memphis calling himself Godfrey Jordan. That suspicion never became a solved lead. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Louise**: Name signed to the roses sent to the funeral. Possibly genuine, possibly staged, still unidentified. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)

### LOCATIONS & SETTING

- **Hotel President, 14th and Baltimore, Kansas City, Missouri**: The central location. In January 1935 it stood in a downtown Kansas City known for vice, nightlife, and political corruption under the Pendergast era. Today the building survives as the Hilton President in the Power & Light District. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; A&E 2025; WDAF/FOX4)
- **Room 1046**: A small interior room, often described as roughly 9 by 12 feet beyond the entry, facing the courtyard rather than the street. That choice matters because Artemus specifically asked for privacy away from outside view. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts from KCPD file; Horner Pt. 1)
- **Muehlebach Hotel**: The nearby hotel where he apparently stayed the previous night as Eugene K. Scott and again chose an interior room. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- **St. Regis Hotel**: Earlier Kansas City lodging where he used the name Duncan Ogletree and shared a room with Donald Kelso. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- **General Hospital, Kansas City**: The hospital where he died shortly after midnight on January 5, 1935. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Mellody-McGilley funeral home**: Held the body during the long identification effort and received the anonymous funeral cash in March 1935. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; user-provided confirmed facts)
- **Memorial Park Cemetery, Kansas City, Kansas**: Burial site, funded by anonymous callers and floral orders rather than family or identified friends. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Wikipedia pointer)
- **Birmingham, Alabama**: Family base from which Ruby tried to trace letters, calls, and rumors after Artemus disappeared. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2)
- **13th and Lydia / 12th and Troost**: Streets associated with Robert Lane's possible encounter with either Ogletree or a connected figure on the night before discovery. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)

### INVESTIGATION, MEDIA & INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE

- Kansas City police treated the death as a homicide almost immediately because the injuries, bindings, and absent weapon did not support suicide. They interviewed hotel staff, nearby guests, and later witnesses from multiple cities. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The identity problem drove the case into national circulation. Police contacted Los Angeles, sent fingerprints to federal authorities, and used newspapers and radio to broadcast the image of the unknown man with the distinctive scar. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- Early press coverage was intense, but as other murders claimed attention, the investigation lost urgency even though leads still trickled in. This helps explain why so many tantalizing threads were never fully resolved. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The funeral reopened media interest. Anonymous money, a staged-looking floral tribute, and phone calls implying private knowledge all made the burial almost as mysterious as the killing. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- Ruby Ogletree did a large share of the later legwork herself. She questioned letters, traced rumors, contacted federal offices, corresponded with Kansas City police, and refused to accept the impossible story that her son was simultaneously dead in Missouri and alive in Egypt. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Later institutions kept the case alive but did not solve it. The FBI reviewed handwriting issues; the KCPD revisited the file into the 1950s; and modern writers such as Horner, Martin Cizmar, and television outlets preserved and reintroduced the record for new audiences. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Horner Pt. 2; WDAF/FOX4; A&E 2025)

### CONFLICTING ACCOUNTS & CONTRADICTIONS

- **Age conflict**: Ruby's 1936 identification materials framed Artemus as having left home at 17 in 1934. Many modern sources call him 19 at death. Hotel staff in 1935 estimated "Owen" anywhere from 20 to 35. The responsible approach is to state the conflict instead of forcing one number as absolute. (Source: AL.com 2018; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Did Artemus speak after discovery?** Later newspaper accounts say he denied being attacked and denied suicide. Kansas City Magazine's review of the original medical affidavit says the affidavit may not mention him speaking at all. Those famous final lines are therefore reported, but disputed. (Source: San Francisco Examiner 1935 via A&E; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Was Robert Lane's passenger really Artemus?** Lane believed so after viewing the body, but Detective Johnson reportedly did not. The timeline is tempting, but the identification is not firm. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Who was the female visitor?** Charles Blocher's account may point to a prostitute or frequent female caller moving through the hotel, but the cited room number was 1026, and later writers note that wire services may have printed that number in error. The connection to Room 1046 is uncertain. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Donald Kelso and Joseph Ogden/Martin**: The New Yorker stated handwriting had linked them, but later FBI review said the Donald Kelso signature on the hotel record was not written by Joseph Ogden. That does not clear Ogden of every possible connection, but it weakens the famous suspect narrative. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Funeral caller details**: Some published accounts preserve the "near my sister" remark, while Kansas City Magazine notes that at least some such details do not survive clearly in the case file. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)

### THEORIES

- **Personal revenge or romantic triangle theory**: This comes directly from the anonymous funeral caller, who said Artemus had been involved with one woman while engaged to another and that cheaters get what is coming to them. Strength: it comes from someone with apparent insider knowledge of the funeral. Weakness: the caller may have been lying, staging a motive, or speaking secondhand. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- **Don Kelso as companion and killer**: The repeated name Don, the note, the phone call, and the St. Regis registration with Donald Kelso make this the most persistent person-centered theory. Strength: Don is tied to multiple parts of the record. Weakness: Don may have been real without being the killer, and no verified full identity was ever produced. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938)
- **Joseph Martin / Joseph Ogden connection**: Because this man used the alias Donald Kelso and was arrested in another body-in-a-trunk killing, Kansas City detectives strongly considered him. Strength: the alias overlap is striking. Weakness: no Ogletree charge followed, and later FBI handwriting review undercut the cleanest evidentiary link. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Joe Simpson involvement or knowledge**: Ruby became convinced that Simpson was the man who called from Memphis as Godfrey Jordan and may have known more than he admitted. Strength: Ruby believed he had first-hand knowledge and reported suspicious statements from him. Weakness: this remains an unproven suspicion from a grieving mother, not a solved lead. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Underworld, hustling, or blackmail milieu**: Because Kansas City in 1935 was a vice-heavy "wide-open town," and because later investigators and period writers used language suggesting sexual or criminal subcultures, some theories place Ogletree in dangerous company. Strength: it fits the aliases, hotel movement, and secrecy. Weakness: many versions rely on innuendo, period prejudice, or speculation rather than hard evidence. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)

### HUMAN COST & AFTERMATH

- The case is often remembered for its strangeness, but at its center was a very young victim who left home full of movement and possibility and ended up buried far from family under a name that was not even his own at the time of death. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2)
- Ruby Ogletree endured a uniquely cruel aftermath: she was not only denied immediate identification of her son, but also received letters and a phone call designed to make her believe he was still alive. That prolonged uncertainty is one of the most painful parts of the case. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The funeral gestures suggest someone cared enough, or wanted to appear to care enough, to prevent a potter's-field burial. Even that act deepened the cruelty by adding theater without truth. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- Because no one was charged, the story never reached moral closure. The result is an enduring cold case in which family grief, police labor, public fascination, and rumor all continue to coexist without resolution. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021; A&E 2025)

### MYTHS VS REALITY

- **Myth:** The victim was never identified. **Reality:** He was identified in 1936 as Artemus Ogletree after Ruby recognized his scar from the American Weekly article and confirmed the match with letters and photographs. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018)
- **Myth:** The case is solved around Donald Kelso. **Reality:** Donald Kelso is a recurring name in the record, but no court ever established who killed Artemus Ogletree. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Myth:** The famous "Nobody" bedside exchange is beyond dispute. **Reality:** It appears in later newspaper accounts, but Kansas City Magazine found that the original medical affidavit may not record him speaking at all. (Source: A&E 2025 citing San Francisco Examiner 1935; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Myth:** The prostitute or female visitor definitely went to Room 1046. **Reality:** The room number in the surviving accounts is disputed, and later writers warn that the link may rest on a wire-service error. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Myth:** The flowers signed Louise prove a lover's identity. **Reality:** They prove only that someone wanted police and the public to think about "Louise," whether that person was Louise or not. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)

### SCRIPT-READY RESEARCH PACK

**VIDEO TITLE**

The Unsolved Murder in Room 1046

**VIDEO TOPIC / CASE NAME**

The 1935 Hotel President / Room 1046 murder of Artemus Ogletree, who checked in under the alias Roland T. Owen, Kansas City, Missouri. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)

**ONE-SENTENCE LOGLINE**

In January 1935, a young traveler using the name Roland T. Owen checked into Kansas City's Hotel President with no luggage, kept his room dark, waited for someone named Don, was found fatally assaulted in Room 1046, and even after his real identity as Artemus Ogletree was uncovered, the case remained unsolved. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)

**WHY THIS STORY WORKS**

- It begins with a perfect documentary hook: a neatly dressed young man arrives alone, asks for an interior room, unpacks only a comb, hairbrush, and toothpaste, and spends the next two days acting as if he is waiting for someone he fears. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; A&E 2025)
- The mystery is layered rather than one-note. There is the false name, the darkened room, the note to "Don," the missing clothes and towels, the strange funeral benefactors, and the later discovery that the victim's mother received letters after her son was already dead. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; AL.com 2018)
- The case is visually rich without needing graphic depiction: the grand downtown hotel, a small interior room with drawn shades, switchboard calls, a bellboy at a locked door, newspaper front pages, a funeral bouquet signed "Love for ever - Louise," and a family in Alabama trying to understand impossible letters. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The victim deserves a careful, humane telling. Artemus Ogletree was not just "the man in Room 1046." He was a son from Florida and Birmingham, one of three children, a young traveler whose mother kept searching for him long after others had given up. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- The story remains unresolved, which gives the ending weight. There are suspects, aliases, suspicious callers, and later investigative theories, but no definitive answer and no one ever charged. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021; WDAF/FOX4)

**CONFIRMED FACTS**

- Artemus Ogletree was born in Florida around 1915 and was one of three children. His family later lived in Birmingham, Alabama. A childhood accident involving hot grease left a large horizontal scar on his scalp above one ear, and the area remained hairless; that scar later became the key to identifying him. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- Ruby Ogletree said Artemus left home in April 1934 at age 17, hitchhiking toward California. The family wired him money, and he sent handwritten updates. Modern retellings often describe him as 19 at death, while hotel staff estimates for "Owen" ranged from about 20 to 35, so his exact age in retellings must be handled as a conflict rather than a settled fact. (Source: AL.com 2018; A&E 2025; Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- At about 1:20 p.m. on January 2, 1935, he checked into the Hotel President at 14th and Baltimore in Kansas City, Missouri, using the name Roland T. Owen and a Los Angeles address. He paid for one night and asked for an interior room several floors up. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025; Wikipedia pointer)
- Bellboy Randolph Propst escorted him to Room 1046, an interior room overlooking the courtyard. Propst later said the guest was neatly dressed in a dark overcoat, had no luggage, and mentioned that he had stayed at the Muehlebach the night before but found the $5 room rate too high. The only items he unpacked were a hairbrush, a comb, and toothpaste from his overcoat pocket. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Hotel maid Mary Soptic encountered him several times. She found the room dark with the shades drawn and said he appeared worried or afraid and "always wanted to kinda keep in the dark." He asked that the room be left unlocked because he was expecting a friend. Later that afternoon she found him dressed on the bed, and on the desk or table was a note reading, "Don, I will be back in fifteen minutes. Wait." (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- On the morning of January 3, Soptic opened the room with her passkey and found him inside even though the door had been locked from the outside. While she worked, she overheard him on the phone saying, "No, Don, I don't want to eat. I am not hungry. I just had breakfast." He also asked whether she covered the whole floor and whether the hotel was residential. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- At about 4 p.m. on January 3, Soptic returned with towels, heard two men inside, and a rough, deep voice, not believed to be Owen's, told her, "We don't need any." That stood out because she knew there were no towels in the room; she had removed them earlier. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- Another guest, Jean Owen of Lee's Summit, stayed in Room 1048 and said she heard loud talking and cursing, with male and female voices, on the floor overnight while her boyfriend Joe Reinert visited from the Midland Flower Shop. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- Elevator operator Charles Blocher reported a woman known to staff as a frequent visitor or "commercial woman" asking about Room 1026, not 1046, and later moving around the hotel with a man. Modern writers note that a wire-service error may have confused 1026 and 1046, so her connection to Artemus Ogletree is uncertain, not proven. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Water department employee Robert Lane said that around 11 p.m. on January 3, near 13th and Lydia, a man in an undershirt, pants, and shoes flagged him down, had a scratch on his arm, seemed to be cupping his hands to hide possible blood, and said he would kill someone "tomorrow." Lane later believed the man was Ogletree, but Detective Ira Johnson reportedly doubted the identification. (Source: Mental Floss; Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- On the morning of January 4, the telephone in Room 1046 was repeatedly found off the hook. Propst first went up around 7 a.m., saw a "Do Not Disturb" sign, heard a deep voice tell him to come in and then to turn on the lights, but could not enter because the door was locked. Around 8:30 a.m., Harold Pike let himself in with a passkey, saw the guest naked on the bed with dark stains on the sheets, assumed he was drunk, reset the phone, and left. Around 10:30 or 11 a.m., Propst returned, entered, and found Ogletree wounded, with blood on his head and blood in the room and bathroom. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Assistant manager M.S. Weaver, hotel staff including Percy Tyrrell, detectives Ira Johnson, William Eldredge, Sgt. Frank Howland, and later D.C. King responded, along with Dr. Harold F. Flanders of General Hospital. When staff tried to re-enter, the door could open only about six inches because Ogletree's body had fallen against it. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts based on KCPD file; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Ogletree had cord tied around his neck, wrists, and ankles; there was bruising suggesting an attempted strangulation; he had multiple stab wounds to the chest, with one puncturing a lung; and he suffered a skull fracture on the right side. Blood was found on the bed, walls, bathroom, and even on the ceiling. Flanders estimated the wounds had likely been inflicted about six to seven hours earlier, often rendered in later summaries as roughly 4 to 5 a.m. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; A&E 2025; Wikipedia pointer)
- Newspapers later reported that when asked who hurt him he answered "Nobody," claimed he fell against the bathtub, and denied trying to kill himself. Kansas City Magazine's 2021 review of the original medical affidavit noted that the affidavit may not record the victim speaking at all, so those lines should be treated as reported in later press, but disputed in the surviving paperwork. (Source: San Francisco Examiner 1935 as cited by A&E; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- He died shortly after midnight on January 5, 1935, at the hospital, after slipping into a coma. Detective Johnson told reporters, "There is no doubt that someone else is mixed up in this." (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- The room was remarkable for what was missing. Investigators found no clothing, no towels, no soap or hotel toiletries, and no knife. What they did find included a necktie label from Botany Worsted Mills of Passaic, New Jersey, a hairpin, a safety pin, an unsmoked cigarette, an unused bottle of dilute sulfuric acid, and two drinking glasses, one broken in the sink. Four small fingerprints on the phone stand were thought possibly to be a woman's, but they were never matched. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- Detectives soon concluded that Roland T. Owen was an alias. The nearby Muehlebach Hotel knew him as Eugene K. Scott of Los Angeles, and the St. Regis Hotel had records for Duncan Ogletree sharing a room with Donald Kelso, a possible "Don." Wrestling promoter Toni Bernardi thought he recognized the victim as a man calling himself Cecil Werner of Omaha, but Omaha promoter Charles Loch did not confirm that identification. Another apparent identification, by Ernest Johnson as his cousin Harvey, collapsed when the family proved the cousin had died years earlier. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- In March 1935, when burial in potter's field was announced, an anonymous male caller asked that the burial be delayed, said cheaters get what is coming to them, and claimed the dead man would be buried near his sister. On March 23, $25 wrapped in newspaper was sent to Mellody-McGilley. A florist received payment requests and later sent 13 American Beauty roses with a card reading "Love for ever - Louise" in disguised handwriting. Reverend E.B. Shively of Roanoke Christian Church officiated. Police attended, and a stakeout of the grave produced nothing. A woman also called the Journal-Post to say the paper was wrong and that "He got into a jam." Ogletree was buried at Memorial Park Cemetery in Kansas City, Kansas. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer; user-provided confirmed facts)
- In the fall of 1936, a friend showed Ruby Ogletree the May 1935 American Weekly story "The Mystery of Room No. 1046," and she recognized her son from the scar photo. On November 2, 1936, after exchanging letters and photographs with Kansas City police, the victim was publicly identified as Artemus Ogletree. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; AL.com 2018; American Weekly May 1935)
- Ruby had already received typewritten letters after her son's death from Chicago and New York, and in August 1935 a caller from Memphis using the name Godfrey Jordan claimed Artemus was alive, married in Cairo, and missing a thumb after a fight. Ruby suspected the caller may actually have been Joe Simpson, the friend who left home with Artemus. She even wrote to federal authorities and the Cairo consul, but no steamship or consular records confirmed an Egypt trip. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; AL.com 2018; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- In 1937, New York police arrested Joseph Martin, also known as Joseph Ogden and Donald Kelso, for the murder of a roommate whose body was shipped in a trunk toward Memphis. The New Yorker later stated Kansas City police linked his handwriting to the letters sent to Ruby, but later FBI review said the signature of Donald Kelso on the hotel record was not written by Joseph Ogden. No charges were filed in the Ogletree case. Detectives reviewed the case into the 1950s, and it remains unsolved. (Source: The New Yorker Jan. 8, 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021; Wikipedia pointer)
- John Arthur Horner disclosed in 2012 that around 2003 he received an out-of-state call from someone who said they had found a shoebox of clippings about the case and an unnamed item mentioned in newspaper coverage among a deceased person's belongings. The caller would not identify the item or themselves. The Hotel President still exists today as the Hilton President. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; WDAF/FOX4; Wikipedia pointer)

**PEOPLE**

- **Artemus Ogletree / Roland T. Owen / Eugene K. Scott / Duncan Ogletree / possibly Cecil Werner**: Victim. Young traveler from Florida and Birmingham whose real name was not publicly known for nearly two years. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- **Ruby Ogletree**: Artemus's mother. Central to the eventual identification. She preserved his letters, recognized the scar from American Weekly, and kept pushing authorities after receiving impossible typewritten letters and the Memphis call. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Mary Soptic**: Hotel maid whose observations provide the clearest portrait of Artemus's final two days alive: the darkness, the fear, the request to leave the door unlocked, the note to Don, and the deep second voice. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Randolph Propst**: Bellboy who escorted Ogletree to Room 1046 and later became one of the first people to witness the aftermath. His recollections frame both the arrival and the discovery. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; A&E 2025)
- **Harold Pike**: Bellboy who entered at about 8:30 a.m. on January 4, saw the guest naked on the bed, assumed he was drunk, and left after resetting the phone. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Jean Owen**: Unrelated guest in Room 1048 who reported hearing men and women loudly talking and cursing on the floor during the crucial night. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- **Joe Reinert**: Jean Owen's boyfriend, associated with Midland Flower Shop, who visited her that night and corroborated her presence. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts; Wikipedia pointer)
- **Charles Blocher**: Elevator operator whose account introduced the mysterious female visitor and another man, though the room-number confusion leaves that lead uncertain. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Robert Lane**: Water department employee who may have encountered either Artemus or a related participant on the night of January 3. His testimony is striking but contested. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Detective Ira Johnson**: Kansas City detective who publicly rejected suicide and stated that someone else was involved. He also reportedly doubted Lane's identification. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- **William Eldredge, Sgt. Frank Howland, D.C. King**: Other investigators named in surviving retellings and file-based summaries. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts based on KCPD file)
- **Dr. Harold F. Flanders**: Physician from General Hospital who examined Ogletree, cut the cords, described the injuries, and estimated they were hours old by the time he arrived. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Donald Kelso / Don**: The most persistent shadow in the case. The name appears in the note, the phone call, and St. Regis records. Whether Don was protector, companion, handler, or killer remains unknown. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- **Joseph Martin / Joseph Ogden**: New York murder suspect later associated with the alias Donald Kelso. The New Yorker and Kansas City police interest made him the most famous later suspect, but the connection did not hold up strongly enough for charges. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Joe Simpson**: Artemus's friend from Alabama who left home with him. Ruby later suspected he was the voice from Memphis calling himself Godfrey Jordan. That suspicion never became a solved lead. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Louise**: Name signed to the roses sent to the funeral. Possibly genuine, possibly staged, still unidentified. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)

**LOCATIONS & SETTING**

- **Hotel President, 14th and Baltimore, Kansas City, Missouri**: The central location. In January 1935 it stood in a downtown Kansas City known for vice, nightlife, and political corruption under the Pendergast era. Today the building survives as the Hilton President in the Power & Light District. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021; A&E 2025; WDAF/FOX4)
- **Room 1046**: A small interior room, often described as roughly 9 by 12 feet beyond the entry, facing the courtyard rather than the street. That choice matters because Artemus specifically asked for privacy away from outside view. (Source: user-provided confirmed facts from KCPD file; Horner Pt. 1)
- **Muehlebach Hotel**: The nearby hotel where he apparently stayed the previous night as Eugene K. Scott and again chose an interior room. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- **St. Regis Hotel**: Earlier Kansas City lodging where he used the name Duncan Ogletree and shared a room with Donald Kelso. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Wikipedia pointer)
- **General Hospital, Kansas City**: The hospital where he died shortly after midnight on January 5, 1935. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Mellody-McGilley funeral home**: Held the body during the long identification effort and received the anonymous funeral cash in March 1935. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; user-provided confirmed facts)
- **Memorial Park Cemetery, Kansas City, Kansas**: Burial site, funded by anonymous callers and floral orders rather than family or identified friends. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Wikipedia pointer)
- **Birmingham, Alabama**: Family base from which Ruby tried to trace letters, calls, and rumors after Artemus disappeared. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2)
- **13th and Lydia / 12th and Troost**: Streets associated with Robert Lane's possible encounter with either Ogletree or a connected figure on the night before discovery. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)

**KEY DOCUMENTS, EVIDENCE & ARTIFACTS**

- Hotel registration card signed "Roland T. Owen" with a Los Angeles address. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- St. Regis and Muehlebach registration records showing other aliases, including Duncan Ogletree and Eugene K. Scott. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2)
- Mary Soptic's witness statements describing the darkness, the fear, the note to Don, and the voice saying no towels were needed. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Telephone evidence: repeated off-hook calls from Room 1046, the note to Don, and the small fingerprints on the phone stand that were never matched. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- Medical findings by Dr. Harold F. Flanders and the autopsy record documenting bindings, stab wounds, punctured lung, skull fracture, and blood evidence suggesting the attack predated discovery by several hours. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Missing-scene evidence: no clothing, no towels, no soap or shampoo, and no knife, all of which suggested outside involvement and deliberate removal of items. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- Physical artifacts found in the room: necktie label from Botany Worsted Mills, hairpin, safety pin, unsmoked cigarette, bottle of dilute sulfuric acid, and two glasses, one broken in the sink. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Funeral artifacts: the $25 wrapped in newspaper, the payment calls from pay phones, and the 13 American Beauty roses with the card "Love for ever - Louise." (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- Ruby Ogletree's handwritten and later typewritten letters, plus later correspondence with police, federal officials, and the Cairo consul. These are crucial because they reveal that someone tried to keep the family believing Artemus was alive. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021; AL.com 2018)
- The May 1935 American Weekly feature and the later identification correspondence of 1936. (Source: American Weekly May 1935; Horner Pt. 2)

**INVESTIGATION, MEDIA & INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE**

- Kansas City police treated the death as a homicide almost immediately because the injuries, bindings, and absent weapon did not support suicide. They interviewed hotel staff, nearby guests, and later witnesses from multiple cities. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The identity problem drove the case into national circulation. Police contacted Los Angeles, sent fingerprints to federal authorities, and used newspapers and radio to broadcast the image of the unknown man with the distinctive scar. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Wikipedia pointer)
- Early press coverage was intense, but as other murders claimed attention, the investigation lost urgency even though leads still trickled in. This helps explain why so many tantalizing threads were never fully resolved. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The funeral reopened media interest. Anonymous money, a staged-looking floral tribute, and phone calls implying private knowledge all made the burial almost as mysterious as the killing. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- Ruby Ogletree did a large share of the later legwork herself. She questioned letters, traced rumors, contacted federal offices, corresponded with Kansas City police, and refused to accept the impossible story that her son was simultaneously dead in Missouri and alive in Egypt. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Later institutions kept the case alive but did not solve it. The FBI reviewed handwriting issues; the KCPD revisited the file into the 1950s; and modern writers such as Horner, Martin Cizmar, and television outlets preserved and reintroduced the record for new audiences. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Horner Pt. 2; WDAF/FOX4; A&E 2025)

**CONFLICTING ACCOUNTS & CONTRADICTIONS**

- **Age conflict**: Ruby's 1936 identification materials framed Artemus as having left home at 17 in 1934. Many modern sources call him 19 at death. Hotel staff in 1935 estimated "Owen" anywhere from 20 to 35. The responsible approach is to state the conflict instead of forcing one number as absolute. (Source: AL.com 2018; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Did Artemus speak after discovery?** Later newspaper accounts say he denied being attacked and denied suicide. Kansas City Magazine's review of the original medical affidavit says the affidavit may not mention him speaking at all. Those famous final lines are therefore reported, but disputed. (Source: San Francisco Examiner 1935 via A&E; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Was Robert Lane's passenger really Artemus?** Lane believed so after viewing the body, but Detective Johnson reportedly did not. The timeline is tempting, but the identification is not firm. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- **Who was the female visitor?** Charles Blocher's account may point to a prostitute or frequent female caller moving through the hotel, but the cited room number was 1026, and later writers note that wire services may have printed that number in error. The connection to Room 1046 is uncertain. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Donald Kelso and Joseph Ogden/Martin**: The New Yorker stated handwriting had linked them, but later FBI review said the Donald Kelso signature on the hotel record was not written by Joseph Ogden. That does not clear Ogden of every possible connection, but it weakens the famous suspect narrative. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Funeral caller details**: Some published accounts preserve the "near my sister" remark, while Kansas City Magazine notes that at least some such details do not survive clearly in the case file. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)

**THEORIES**

- **Personal revenge or romantic triangle theory**: This comes directly from the anonymous funeral caller, who said Artemus had been involved with one woman while engaged to another and that cheaters get what is coming to them. Strength: it comes from someone with apparent insider knowledge of the funeral. Weakness: the caller may have been lying, staging a motive, or speaking secondhand. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- **Don Kelso as companion and killer**: The repeated name Don, the note, the phone call, and the St. Regis registration with Donald Kelso make this the most persistent person-centered theory. Strength: Don is tied to multiple parts of the record. Weakness: Don may have been real without being the killer, and no verified full identity was ever produced. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938)
- **Joseph Martin / Joseph Ogden connection**: Because this man used the alias Donald Kelso and was arrested in another body-in-a-trunk killing, Kansas City detectives strongly considered him. Strength: the alias overlap is striking. Weakness: no Ogletree charge followed, and later FBI handwriting review undercut the cleanest evidentiary link. (Source: The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Joe Simpson involvement or knowledge**: Ruby became convinced that Simpson was the man who called from Memphis as Godfrey Jordan and may have known more than he admitted. Strength: Ruby believed he had first-hand knowledge and reported suspicious statements from him. Weakness: this remains an unproven suspicion from a grieving mother, not a solved lead. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Underworld, hustling, or blackmail milieu**: Because Kansas City in 1935 was a vice-heavy "wide-open town," and because later investigators and period writers used language suggesting sexual or criminal subcultures, some theories place Ogletree in dangerous company. Strength: it fits the aliases, hotel movement, and secrecy. Weakness: many versions rely on innuendo, period prejudice, or speculation rather than hard evidence. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)

**OPEN QUESTIONS**

- Who was "Don," and was Don the same person as Donald Kelso? (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2)
- Why did Artemus insist on dark rooms, interior rooms, and an unlocked door if he was only casually expecting company? (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- Who removed the clothing, towels, toiletries, and likely weapon from Room 1046? (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- Were the small fingerprints on the phone stand from a woman connected to the assault, an earlier guest, or an unrelated contact? (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Mental Floss)
- Was Robert Lane's late-night passenger actually Ogletree, someone involved in the crime, or an unrelated injured man? (Source: Horner Pt. 1)
- Why would someone go to the trouble of sending money, flowers, and misleading letters rather than simply vanish? (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- Did the funeral callers and the later Memphis caller come from the same person or group? (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- What was the unnamed item in the shoebox mentioned to John Horner around 2003? (Source: Horner Pt. 2; WDAF/FOX4)

**HUMAN COST & AFTERMATH**

- The case is often remembered for its strangeness, but at its center was a very young victim who left home full of movement and possibility and ended up buried far from family under a name that was not even his own at the time of death. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2)
- Ruby Ogletree endured a uniquely cruel aftermath: she was not only denied immediate identification of her son, but also received letters and a phone call designed to make her believe he was still alive. That prolonged uncertainty is one of the most painful parts of the case. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The funeral gestures suggest someone cared enough, or wanted to appear to care enough, to prevent a potter's-field burial. Even that act deepened the cruelty by adding theater without truth. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)
- Because no one was charged, the story never reached moral closure. The result is an enduring cold case in which family grief, police labor, public fascination, and rumor all continue to coexist without resolution. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021; A&E 2025)

**MYTHS VS REALITY**

- **Myth:** The victim was never identified. **Reality:** He was identified in 1936 as Artemus Ogletree after Ruby recognized his scar from the American Weekly article and confirmed the match with letters and photographs. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; AL.com 2018)
- **Myth:** The case is solved around Donald Kelso. **Reality:** Donald Kelso is a recurring name in the record, but no court ever established who killed Artemus Ogletree. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Myth:** The famous "Nobody" bedside exchange is beyond dispute. **Reality:** It appears in later newspaper accounts, but Kansas City Magazine found that the original medical affidavit may not record him speaking at all. (Source: A&E 2025 citing San Francisco Examiner 1935; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Myth:** The prostitute or female visitor definitely went to Room 1046. **Reality:** The room number in the surviving accounts is disputed, and later writers warn that the link may rest on a wire-service error. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- **Myth:** The flowers signed Louise prove a lover's identity. **Reality:** They prove only that someone wanted police and the public to think about "Louise," whether that person was Louise or not. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; Mental Floss)

**GAPS & UNCERTAINTY LOG**

- The precise age Artemus should be described as at death remains contested across family statements, modern summaries, and witness estimates. (Source: AL.com 2018; A&E 2025; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The reported hospital dialogue beginning with "Nobody" is famous but not cleanly preserved in the original affidavit, so it should never be narrated as undisputed fact. (Source: Kansas City Magazine 2021; San Francisco Examiner 1935 as cited later)
- The roles of the female visitor, Don Kelso, Joseph Ogden, Joe Simpson, and "Louise" overlap in suggestive ways but never join into a fully proven chain of evidence. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; The New Yorker 1938)
- Some details in popular retellings appear to have hardened from repetition rather than from fresh archival proof. When possible, file-based versions from Horner and Kansas City Magazine should outrank dramatized or unsourced retellings. (Source: Horner Pt. 1; Horner Pt. 2; Kansas City Magazine 2021)
- The 2003 shoebox tip remains tantalizing but unverifiable in public. The item allegedly found with the clippings has never been disclosed. (Source: Horner Pt. 2; WDAF/FOX4)
- The exact chain of Artemus's travels from leaving Birmingham to dying in Kansas City is still incomplete, especially regarding California, companions, and why he used so many names. (Source: AL.com 2018; Horner Pt. 2)
